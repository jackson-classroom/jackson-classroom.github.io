An object moving in a circle at a steady speed keeps changing direction, so its velocity keeps changing. That means it's accelerating, toward the center of the circle. And whenever something accelerates, a net force points the same way.

Velocity in a circle

Uniform circular motion means moving in a circle at a constant speed: a ball whirled on a string, a car going around a roundabout, a kid on a merry-go-round.

Velocity is a vector. It has a size (the speed) and a direction. In a circle, the speed can stay the same while the direction changes every instant. So the velocity is always changing.

along the circle

Which way?

At every instant, the velocity points along the circle, the way the object is moving. That line just touches the circle, at a right angle to the radius. It's called the tangent.

v = 2πr / T

How fast?

Speed is distance divided by time. In one trip around, the object travels the circumference, 2πr, in one period, T.

Worked example

A kid sits 2 m from the center of a merry-go-round. It goes around once every 4 s. How fast is the kid moving?

v = 2πr / T
v = 2π × 2 m / 4 s
v = 3.14 m/s

Direction: along the circle, the way the merry-go-round turns. The speed stays 3.14 m/s, but the direction changes every instant.

Let go, and it goes straight

If the string breaks, nothing pulls the ball toward the center anymore. By Newton's first law, it keeps the velocity it had at that instant, so it flies off in a straight line along the tangent. It doesn't fly straight outward, and it doesn't keep curving. You can try it in Swing the ball below.

Use the radius, not the diameter. r is measured from the center of the circle to the object.

Centripetal acceleration

Acceleration is any change in velocity. That includes a change in direction, not just speeding up or slowing down. An object moving in a circle is always changing direction, so it is always accelerating, even when its speed is steady.

Which way? Toward the center.

Look at the velocity at two moments, 1 and 2. The arrows are the same length (same speed) but point different ways.

Put them tail to tail. The arrow from the tip of v1 to the tip of v2 is the change in velocity, Δv. It points toward the center of the circle. Acceleration points the same way as Δv, so the acceleration points toward the center too.

That's why it's called centripetal acceleration. "Centripetal" means "center-seeking."

Centripetal accelerationAn object moving in a circle accelerates toward the center. The faster it goes and the tighter the circle, the bigger the acceleration.
ac = v² / r

Worked example

The kid on the merry-go-round from above is moving at 3.14 m/s, 2 m from the center.

ac = v² / r
ac = (3.14 m/s)² / 2 m
ac = 4.93 m/s² toward the center

The speed is squared

Because v is squared, the speed has a big effect. The radius is on the bottom, so a bigger circle means less acceleration.

SpeedRadiusacCompared to row 1

Square the speed first. v² / r means (v × v) / r. Dividing v by r and stopping there is the most common mistake.

The net force points to the center

Newton's second law says the net force and the acceleration always point the same way. In a circle the acceleration points toward the center, so the net force points toward the center too. Without it, the object would go straight (Newton's first law).

Newton's second law, in a circleTo move in a circle, an object needs a net force pointing toward the center.
Fnet = m · ac   (toward the center)

The 40 kg kid on the merry-go-round: Fnet = 40 kg × 4.93 m/s² = 197.2 N, toward the center.

Where does the net force come from?

Something real has to pull or push the object toward the center. It's always one of the forces from Unit 3.

Moving in a circleThe force pointing toward the center
A ball whirled on a stringFtens the string pulls the ball in
A car on a flat curveFfrict the road grips the tires sideways
The Moon going around EarthFgrav Earth's gravity pulls the Moon in
A rider in a spinning carnival rideFnorm the wall pushes the rider in

Worked example: a car on a curve

A 1200 kg car drives around a flat curve with a radius of 50 m at 15 m/s. Seen from behind, the center of the curve is to the left.

DirectionAdd upTotal
Vertical11760 N up − 11760 N down0 N
Sidewaysfriction only5400 N left
ac = v² / r = (15 m/s)² / 50 m = 4.5 m/s²
Fnet = m · ac = 1200 kg × 4.5 m/s² = 5400 N

The vertical forces cancel. Friction is the only force left, so friction has to supply all 5400 N, pointing toward the center.

"Centripetal force" isn't a new kind of force. You may hear the net force in a circle called the centripetal force. It's just a name for whatever real force (tension, friction, gravity, or the normal force) points toward the center. Never add an extra "centripetal" arrow to a free-body diagram.

There is no outward force

When a car turns left, you feel thrown to the right, into the door. Nothing is pushing you outward. Your body is trying to keep going straight (inertia) while the car turns left underneath you, so the door runs into you. Then the door pushes you inward, toward the center of the turn, and that's what makes you turn with the car.

Watch-outs

  • Constant speed means no acceleration.In a circle the direction keeps changing, so the velocity keeps changing. That's an acceleration, toward the center.
  • The acceleration points the way the object is moving.That's the velocity. In uniform circular motion the acceleration points toward the center, at a right angle to the velocity.
  • Centrifugal force pushes things outward.There is no outward force. You feel thrown outward because your body keeps trying to go straight while the car or the ride turns.
  • When the string breaks, the ball flies straight out, away from the center.It flies off along the tangent, in a straight line, the way it was moving at that instant.
  • Centripetal force is one more force to draw on the free-body diagram.It's the net force: a name for whatever real force points toward the center. Draw the tension, friction, gravity, or normal force, never an extra "centripetal" arrow.
  • Going twice as fast takes twice the force.The speed is squared. Twice the speed means four times the acceleration, so four times the net force.
