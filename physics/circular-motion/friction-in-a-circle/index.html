What the turn needs. What the road can give.

When a car turns on a flat road, friction from the road is the only thing pushing it toward the center. Every problem on this page comes down to two numbers: how much friction the turn needs, and how much the road can give.

Friction does the turning

Picture a car driving around a flat curve at a steady speed. Look at it from behind, so the center of the curve is off to the left. Three forces act on it:

  • Fgrav pulls it down.
  • Fnorm from the road pushes it up. The car isn't moving up or down, so these two cancel: Fnorm = m · g.
  • Ffrict from the road on the tires pushes it sideways, toward the center.

Friction is the only force pointing toward the center, so friction is the net force. From page 1, the net force in a circle is m · v² / r. Put those together:

Car on a flat curveThe friction from the road is the net force that turns the car.
Ffrict = m · v² / r

Worked example: find the friction

A 1200 kg car drives around a flat curve with a radius of 50 m at 15 m/s. Find the friction force Ffrict on the car.

Ffrict = m · v² / r
Ffrict = 1200 kg × (15 m/s)² / 50 m
Ffrict = 1200 × 225 / 50 = 5400 N

The road pushes the tires toward the center with 5400 N. That's the whole net force. The normal force and gravity (11760 N each) cancel.

Which friction is it? The tires roll along the road, but they don't slide sideways. So this is static friction, the kind from Unit 3 that is as big as it needs to be, up to a limit. That limit is μ · Fnorm.

How much friction does the turn need?

The friction a turn needs is m · v² / r. Two things make that bigger: going faster and turning tighter (a smaller radius).

Speed is squared. Double your speed and the turn needs four times the friction, not twice. That's why a curve that feels easy at 25 mph can send you off the road at 50 mph.

How much friction can the road give?

Static friction can only get so big. The most it can give is μ · Fnorm, and on a flat road Fnorm = m · g. The coefficient μ depends on the tires and the road surface.

Look back at the first table. Turning at 20 m/s on a 50 m curve needs 9600 N. That's just a bit more than even a dry road can give.

μ multiplies a force, not a mass. Find Fnorm = m · g in newtons first, then multiply by μ.

Will it make the turn?

Find both numbers and compare them.

needed ≤ most it can give

The car makes the turn

Static friction gives exactly what the turn needs, m · v² / r, and the car follows the curve.

needed > most it can give

The car slides

The road can't push hard enough. The car can't turn as tightly as the road does, so it slides toward the outside of the curve.

Worked example: will it slide?

A 1000 kg car takes a flat curve with a radius of 25 m at 15 m/s. The road is wet, μ = 0.5. Does it make the turn?

Needed: m · v² / r = 1000 × 15² / 25 = 9000 N
Can give: Fnorm = 1000 × 9.8 = 9800 N
μ · Fnorm = 0.5 × 9800 = 4900 N
9000 N > 4900 N, so it slides.

Slow down to 10 m/s and the turn only needs 1000 × 10² / 25 = 4000 N. That's less than 4900 N, so the car makes it.

Nothing throws the car outward. When it slides, there just isn't enough friction to pull it around the curve. It goes straighter than the road does, and that carries it to the outside.

Three more problems

Every problem uses the same two equations. The only thing that changes is which number you're asked to find.

  1. Find Fnorm = m · g.On a flat road, the normal force is the weight.
  2. Find the friction. Either from the turn (m · v² / r), or from the road (μ · Fnorm).
  3. Set Ffrict = m · v² / r and solve for whatever is missing.

Find the smallest μ

A 1000 kg car drives around a flat curve with a radius of 40 m at 12 m/s. Find the smallest μ that keeps it from sliding.

Ffrict = m · v² / r = 1000 × 12² / 40 = 3600 N
Fnorm = m · g = 1000 × 9.8 = 9800 N
μ = Ffrict / Fnorm = 3600 / 9800 = 0.37

Any road with μ of at least 0.37 works. A wet road (0.5) is fine. A snowy road (0.3) isn't.

Find the fastest speed

A 1500 kg car drives on a flat curve with a radius of 30 m. The coefficient of friction is 0.6. Find the fastest speed v it can have without sliding.

Fnorm = 1500 × 9.8 = 14700 N
Ffrict = μ · Fnorm = 0.6 × 14700 = 8820 N
8820 = 1500 × v² / 30
v² = 8820 × 30 / 1500 = 176.4
v = √176.4 = 13.28 m/s

Solving for v: multiply by r, divide by m, then take the square root. Don't stop at v².

Find the tightest curve

A 1200 kg car is going 20 m/s on a dry road, μ = 0.8. Find the smallest radius r it can turn on without sliding.

Fnorm = 1200 × 9.8 = 11760 N
Ffrict = μ · Fnorm = 0.8 × 11760 = 9408 N
9408 = 1200 × 20² / r
r = 1200 × 400 / 9408 = 51.02 m

A tighter curve (smaller r) needs more friction than the road can give. A wider one is fine.

Watch-outs

  • Friction always points backward, against the motion.On a turn at a steady speed, the road's friction on the tires points sideways, toward the center of the curve. That's what turns the car.
  • A force throws the car to the outside when it skids.Nothing pushes it out. There just isn't enough friction to pull it around, so it goes straighter than the curve and ends up on the outside.
  • The friction on a turning car is always μ · Fnorm.That's the most the road can give. The friction on a car that makes the turn is exactly what it needs: m · v² / r.
  • Twice as fast needs twice the friction.Speed is squared. Twice as fast needs four times the friction.
  • The most friction is μ × mass.μ multiplies the normal force in newtons. A 1000 kg car with μ = 0.5 can get 0.5 × 9800 N = 4900 N, not 500 N.
  • You found v², so you're done.Take the square root. A car going 176 m/s around a curve would be going almost 400 mph.
