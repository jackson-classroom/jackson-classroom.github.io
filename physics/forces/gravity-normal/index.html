The force of gravity

The force of gravity (Fgrav) is Earth pulling on an object. It's also called the object's weight. It always points straight down, and it's on the free-body diagram of anything near Earth, whether it's sitting still, sliding, or flying through the air.

Force of gravity (weight)An object's weight is its mass times the strength of gravity.
Fgrav = m · g

g is the same for every object. A bowling ball has a bigger weight than a marble because it has more mass, not because gravity is stronger for it. In our problems, use g = 9.8 m/s² unless the problem says otherwise.

Mass and weight are not the same thing

m, in kg

Mass

How much stuff is in an object. It's the same everywhere: on Earth, on the Moon, or floating in space.

A 60 kg student is 60 kg everywhere.
Fgrav, in N

Weight

A force: how hard gravity pulls on that mass. It changes if g changes.

On Earth, the 60 kg student weighs 60 × 9.8 = 588 N.

The normal force

The normal force (Fnorm) is a surface pushing back on an object that's pressing on it. "Normal" means perpendicular: the push points straight out of the surface. A floor or table pushes straight up. A wall pushes straight out from the wall, sideways.

There's no equation like Fgrav = m · g for the normal force. The surface pushes back with whatever it takes to keep the object from sinking into it. You find Fnorm from the free-body diagram: if the object isn't moving up or down, the vertical forces have to cancel.

Fnorm = Fgrav

Just resting

Nothing else pushes up or down, so the surface holds up exactly the weight.

A 98 N box on the floor → Fnorm = 98 N
Fnorm = Fgrav + push

Pushed down

Your push points the same way as gravity, so the surface has to push back harder.

98 N box, pushed down with 20 N → Fnorm = 118 N
Fnorm = Fgrav − pull

Pulled up (but not lifted)

Your pull holds up part of the weight, so the surface doesn't have to push as hard.

98 N box, pulled up with 30 N → Fnorm = 68 N
Fnorm = Fgrav

Pushed sideways

A sideways force doesn't push into the floor or lift off it, so it doesn't change Fnorm.

98 N cart, pushed sideways with 40 N → Fnorm = 98 N

The method

  1. Find Fgrav. Fgrav = m × 9.8.
  2. List every other up or down force. A push down, a pull up, a rope.Ignore left–right forces. They don't change Fnorm on a level surface.
  3. Make up = down. Fnorm is whatever up-force is missing so the vertical forces cancel.

Worked example

A 5 kg box sits on a table. You press straight down on it with 20 N.

StepWork
Fgrav5 kg × 9.8 = 49 N down
Down49 N + 20 N = 69 N
Up = downFnorm = 69 N up

Fnorm = 69 N. More than the box's weight, because the table also holds up your push.

No contact, no normal force. Fnorm only exists where an object touches a surface. A ball in the air, a skydiver, or a lamp hanging from a chain has no normal force on its free-body diagram.

Watch-outs

  • The normal force is always equal to the weight.Only when nothing else pushes up or down. Push down on a box and Fnorm gets bigger. Pull up on it and Fnorm gets smaller.
  • Mass and weight are the same thing.Mass is in kg and stays the same everywhere. Weight (Fgrav) is a force in N and depends on g. An astronaut has the same mass on the Moon, but a much smaller weight.
  • Heavier objects have a bigger g.g = 9.8 m/s² for everything on Earth. A heavier object has a bigger weight because it has more mass: Fgrav = m · g.
  • The normal force always points up.It points straight out of the surface. A floor pushes up, but a wall pushes sideways. Press a book flat against a wall and the wall's Fnorm points away from the wall.
  • Every free-body diagram has a normal force.Fnorm needs contact with a surface. A thrown ball, a skydiver, or a lamp hanging from a chain has Fgrav but no Fnorm.
