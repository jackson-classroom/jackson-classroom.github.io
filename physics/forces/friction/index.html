Static vs. kinetic friction

Friction (Ffrict) is a force from a surface that resists sliding. It acts along the surface, opposite the way the object slides, or the way it would slide. There are two kinds, and one question tells them apart: are the two surfaces sliding across each other?

not sliding

Static friction

Pushes back exactly as hard as it needs to so nothing slides, up to a maximum. Push harder, and it pushes back harder.

Push a heavy box with 30 N and it doesn't move → friction is 30 N. Push with 45 N and it still doesn't move → friction is 45 N.
sliding

Kinetic friction

Stays the same size no matter how hard you push or how fast the object slides. It's set by the surfaces and how hard they press together.

Once the box slides, friction stays at μ · Fnorm, whether you push with 60 N or 100 N.

Breaking free

Static friction can only get so big. Push harder than the maximum static friction and the object breaks free and starts sliding. Then kinetic friction takes over, and kinetic friction is usually smaller than that maximum.

That's why it's harder to get something moving than to keep it moving. To start a heavy couch sliding, you have to beat the maximum static friction. Once it's sliding, only the smaller kinetic friction pushes back.

Calculating the force of friction

In our problems, "force of friction" means the friction on something that's sliding, and there's one equation for it.

Force of frictionThe force of friction is the coefficient of friction times the normal force.
Ffrict = μ · Fnorm
SymbolMeansUnit
Ffrictforce of frictionN
μcoefficient of friction (say "mew"). How grippy the two surfaces are.no unit
Fnormnormal force. How hard the surfaces press together.N

μ has no unit because it's one force divided by another. It's usually between 0 and 1: the bigger it is, the grippier the surfaces. (Other books may write μk for this. In our class it's just μ.)

Surfacesμ (about)
Rubber tire on dry concrete0.7
Rubber tire on wet concrete0.5
Wood on wood0.3
Waxed ski on snow0.1
Shoes on ice0.05

Finding the force of friction

  1. Find the normal force. On a level floor, Fnorm = Fgrav = m × 9.8.If the problem gives the weight in N, that's already Fgrav. (This only works when nothing else pushes up or down on the object. Page 3 has more on Fnorm.)
  2. Multiply by μ. Ffrict = μ · Fnorm.μ multiplies a force in N, never the mass in kg.
  3. Give the direction. Friction points opposite the way the object slides.

Worked example

A 30 kg crate is shoved and slides to the right across a wooden floor. μ = 0.3.

  1. Step 1 · Normal forceFnorm = Fgrav = 30 kg × 9.8
    Fnorm = 294 N
  2. Step 2 · FrictionFfrict = μ · Fnorm = 0.3 × 294 N
    Ffrict = 88.2 N left

Working backward: finding μ

If a problem gives you the friction and asks for μ, divide: μ = Ffrict / Fnorm. A common setup: a steady 90 N push slides a 300 N dresser at a constant speed. Constant speed means the forces are balanced, so Ffrict = 90 N, and μ = 90 N / 300 N = 0.3.

Watch-outs

  • Friction is always μ · Fnorm.That's only for something that's sliding. Before it slides, static friction just matches the push. Push a box with 20 N and it doesn't move? Friction is 20 N, whatever μ · Fnorm works out to.
  • It didn't move, so my push must be smaller than the friction.Your push and the static friction are equal. That's why Fnet = 0 and nothing moves. Push harder and static friction grows to match, until you pass its maximum.
  • Push harder on a sliding box and the friction gets bigger.Once it's sliding, friction stays at μ · Fnorm no matter how hard you push or how fast it goes. The extra push makes the box accelerate.
  • More surface touching means more friction.Friction depends on the two surfaces (μ) and how hard they press together (Fnorm), not on how much area touches. A brick slides about the same on its wide face or its narrow side.
  • Ffrict = μ × mass.μ multiplies a force. Find Fnorm first: on a level floor, Fnorm = m × 9.8. A 10 kg box with μ = 0.2 has Ffrict = 0.2 × 98 N = 19.6 N, not 2 N.
