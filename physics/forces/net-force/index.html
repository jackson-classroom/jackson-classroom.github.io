Find the net force. Then find the acceleration.

The net force is what's left over after you add up every force on an object. Newton's second law says that leftover force is what makes the object accelerate: a = Fnet / m.

Determining the net force

The net force (Fnet) is the sum of all the forces acting on an object, taking direction into account. Forces are vectors, so you can't just add up every number on the diagram.

same way → add

Forces in the same direction

Add them together.

30 N right and 20 N right → 50 N right
opposite ways → subtract

Forces in opposite directions

Subtract the smaller from the bigger. The net force points the way of the bigger side.

50 N right and 20 N left → 30 N right

The method

  1. Add the vertical forces. Total up minus total down.
  2. Add the horizontal forces. Total right minus total left.
  3. Whatever doesn't cancel is the net force. Give its size and direction, like "30 N right."If both directions cancel, Fnet = 0 N and the forces are balanced (page 1).

Worked example

A skydiver just after jumping, before she's picked up much speed.

DirectionAdd upTotal
Vertical300 N up − 700 N down400 N down
Horizontalno horizontal forces0 N

Fnet = 400 N down. Gravity is bigger, so the net force points down.

Reading the diagrams on this page: up–down arrows and left–right arrows are each drawn to their own scale, so compare arrows within a direction. Go by the labeled numbers, not the arrow lengths.

Newton's second law

Newton's second lawAn object's acceleration points in the direction of the net force. It gets bigger when the net force is bigger, and smaller when the mass is bigger.
a = Fnet / m   or   Fnet = m · a

Page 1 said an unbalanced force changes an object's velocity. Newton's second law tells you how fast the velocity changes. That's the acceleration.

SymbolMeansUnit
Fnetnet force (not just one of the forces)N (newtons)
mmasskg
aaccelerationm/s²

A newton is defined by this law: 1 N = 1 kg · m/s². One newton is the net force that gives a 1 kg object an acceleration of 1 m/s².

Finding the acceleration from a free-body diagram

  1. Find the net force. Add up each direction, just like Practice 1. Get its size and direction.
  2. Divide by the mass. a = Fnet / m, with the mass in kg.Use the mass, not the weight. Fgrav is a force in newtons.
  3. Give the direction. The acceleration points the same way as the net force.

More force, more acceleration. More mass, less acceleration.

Use the net force. If a diagram has more than one force, find Fnet first. Plugging in just the push or just the pull gives the wrong acceleration.

Watch-outs

  • The net force is all the forces added together.Only forces pointing the same way add. Forces pointing opposite ways subtract. 50 N right and 20 N left is 30 N right, not 70 N.
  • The net force is the biggest force on the diagram.The biggest force is usually only part of the story. Something else almost always pushes back against it.
  • Use the push (or pull) in F = ma.Use Fnet. A 10 kg box pushed with 50 N against 30 N of friction has Fnet = 20 N, so a = 2 m/s², not 5 m/s².
  • Mass and weight are the same thing.Mass is in kg. Weight (Fgrav) is a force in N. A 10 kg box weighs 98 N. Use the mass in kg when you divide.
  • Fnet = 0 means the object is standing still.Fnet = 0 means a = 0. The velocity isn't changing, so the object could be at rest or moving at a steady speed.
