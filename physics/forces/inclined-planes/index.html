Tilt your axes

On a level floor, we sorted every force into up–down and left–right. On a ramp, the box doesn't move straight down or straight sideways. It slides along the ramp. So we tilt our axes to line up with the ramp.

∥ parallel

Along the ramp

The direction the box slides: down the ramp, or up it.

On this axis: Fgrav∥ (down the ramp), and Ffrict (up the ramp) if there's friction.
⊥ perpendicular

Straight into or out of the ramp

At a right angle to the ramp's surface. The box never moves this way. It doesn't sink into the ramp or float off it.

On this axis: Fnorm (out of the ramp) and Fgrav⊥ (into the ramp).

Fnorm points straight out of the ramp, so it's already on the ⊥ axis. Friction runs along the surface, so it's already on the ∥ axis.

Gravity is the only force that doesn't line up. It points straight down, tilted compared to both axes. So we split it into two pieces, one on each axis. It's the same idea as breaking a vector into x and y components, just with tilted axes.

Splitting gravity

Find Fgrav the usual way (Fgrav = m · g), then split it using the angle of the ramp.

Splitting gravity on a rampThe piece along the ramp uses sine. The piece into the ramp uses cosine.
Fgrav∥ = Fgrav · sin θ
Fgrav⊥ = Fgrav · cos θ
SymbolMeansUnit
Fgrav∥the piece of gravity parallel to the ramp. It pulls the object down the ramp.N
Fgrav⊥the piece of gravity perpendicular to the ramp. It presses the object into the ramp.N
Fgravthe whole force of gravity, m · gN
θthe angle of the ramp, measured up from level ground (say "theta")degrees

Why sin and cos?

Fgrav and its two pieces make a right triangle, with Fgrav as the hypotenuse. The angle between Fgrav and Fgrav⊥ is the same angle θ as the ramp. Tilt the ramp more, and the ⊥ direction tilts away from straight down by the same amount.

  • cosFgrav⊥ is next to θ (adjacent), so Fgrav⊥ = Fgrav · cos θ.
  • sinFgrav∥ is across from θ (opposite), so Fgrav∥ = Fgrav · sin θ.

On a level floor (0°), nothing pulls the box along, and sin 0° = 0. All of gravity presses into the floor, so Fgrav⊥ = Fgrav, just like page 3. Sliding down a ramp

Once gravity is split, a ramp problem works just like a level-floor problem, one axis at a time. Start with a slippery ramp: no friction.

  1. Find Fgrav. Fgrav = m × 9.8.
  2. Split it. Fgrav∥ = Fgrav · sin θ and Fgrav⊥ = Fgrav · cos θ.
  3. Perpendicular axis: Fnorm = Fgrav⊥. The box doesn't sink into the ramp or lift off it, so the ⊥ forces cancel.On a ramp, Fnorm is less than Fgrav.
  4. Parallel axis: Fnet = Fgrav∥. With no friction, Fgrav∥ is the only force along the ramp.Fnet points down the ramp.
  5. a = Fnet / m. Same as page 2.

Worked example

A 10 kg box slides down a frictionless ramp tilted at 30°.

  1. Step 1 · GravityFgrav = 10 kg × 9.8 = 98 N
  2. Step 2 · Split itFgrav∥ = 98 N × sin 30° = 49 N
    Fgrav⊥ = 98 N × cos 30° = 84.87 N
  3. Step 3 · Normal forceFnorm = Fgrav⊥ = 84.87 N
  4. Step 4 · Net forceFnet = Fgrav∥ = 49 N down the ramp
  5. Step 5 · Accelerationa = 49 N / 10 kg = 4.9 m/s² down the ramp
Without friction, the mass doesn't matter. A heavier box has a bigger Fgrav∥, but it also has more mass to speed up, and the two cancel: a = 9.8 × sin θ for every object. A 2 kg box and a 20 kg box slide down the same frictionless ramp side by side.

Sliding down with friction

If the ramp isn't slippery, friction acts on the sliding object too. It points up the ramp, opposite the sliding. It's the same equation as page 4, Ffrict = μ · Fnorm. The only change is that on a ramp, Fnorm = Fgrav⊥.

Friction on a rampFriction uses the normal force, and on a ramp that's the perpendicular piece of gravity.
Ffrict = μ · Fnorm = μ · Fgrav⊥
  1. Find Fgrav and split it. Same as before.
  2. Fnorm = Fgrav⊥. Same as before.
  3. Ffrict = μ · Fnorm. It points up the ramp.μ multiplies Fnorm, not Fgrav and not the mass.
  4. Fnet = Fgrav∥ − Ffrict. Fgrav∥ pulls down the ramp and friction pushes back up it, so subtract.
  5. a = Fnet / m.

Worked example

A 20 kg sled slides down a snowy hill tilted at 30°. μ = 0.1.

  1. Step 1 · Gravity, splitFgrav = 20 kg × 9.8 = 196 N
    Fgrav∥ = 196 N × sin 30° = 98 N
    Fgrav⊥ = 196 N × cos 30° = 169.74 N
  2. Step 2 · FrictionFnorm = Fgrav⊥ = 169.74 N
    Ffrict = 0.1 × 169.74 N = 16.97 N up the hill
  3. Step 3 · Net forceFnet = 98 N − 16.97 N = 81.03 N down the hill
  4. Step 4 · Accelerationa = 81.03 N / 20 kg = 4.05 m/s² down the hill

Without friction, the sled would speed up at 9.8 × sin 30° = 4.9 m/s². Friction takes some of that away. Every object in our ramp problems is already sliding down, so this is kinetic friction, just like page 4.

Watch-outs

  • On a ramp, Fnorm = Fgrav.Only on a level floor. On a ramp, the surface only has to hold back the piece of gravity pressing into it: Fnorm = Fgrav⊥ = Fgrav · cos θ, which is less than Fgrav.
  • Fgrav∥ uses cos and Fgrav⊥ uses sin.It's the other way around. Check with a level floor (θ = 0°): nothing pulls the box along, and sin 0° = 0. So the piece along the ramp uses sin.
  • On a ramp, gravity points into the ramp (or down the ramp).Gravity always points straight down. Fgrav∥ and Fgrav⊥ are two pieces of it. Once you split Fgrav, use the pieces instead of Fgrav, not on top of it, or you'll count gravity twice.
  • sin 30 = −0.988.If your calculator says that, it's in radians mode. Switch it to degrees. sin 30° should be exactly 0.5.
  • A heavier box slides down a ramp faster.Without friction, a = 9.8 × sin θ for every object. More mass means a bigger Fgrav∥, but also more mass to speed up, and the two cancel.
  • A steeper ramp pushes back harder.Steeper means cos θ gets smaller, so Fgrav⊥ and the normal force get smaller. On a vertical wall, the box isn't pressing on the surface at all.
