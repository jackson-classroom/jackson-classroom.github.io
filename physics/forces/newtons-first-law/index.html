Newton's first law

Newton's first law · the law of inertiaAn object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and direction, unless acted on by an unbalanced force.

Inertia is an object's tendency to keep doing what it's already doing. A force isn't needed to keep something moving, only to change how it moves. The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has.

That means a free-body diagram tells you a lot about motion. You can't tell from the forces whether an object is moving, but you can tell whether its motion is changing.

Fnet = 0

Balanced forces

The forces cancel in both directions. The velocity doesn't change: the object stays at rest, or keeps moving at a constant speed in a straight line.

A book sitting on a desk. A car on cruise control on a straight highway.
Fnet ≠ 0

Unbalanced forces

The forces don't cancel in at least one direction. The velocity changes: the object speeds up, slows down, or changes direction.

A sprinter leaving the blocks. A car braking at a red light.

Reading a free-body diagram

A free-body diagram (FBD) strips a situation down to one object, drawn as a box, and the forces acting on it, drawn as arrows. Each arrow points the way the force pushes or pulls, is labeled with its name and size in newtons (N), and is drawn longer for bigger forces. (On this page, up–down arrows and left–right arrows are each drawn to their own scale, so compare arrows within a direction.)

ForceSymbolWhere it comes from
Gravity (weight)FgEarth pulling down on the object. Always there, always straight down.
Normal forceFNA surface pushing back on an object resting on it, straight out from the surface.
FrictionFfA surface resisting sliding. Points opposite the motion (or the attempted motion).
TensionFTA rope, string, cable, or chain pulling. Always a pull, along the rope.
Applied forceFappA person or another object pushing or pulling directly.
Air resistanceFairAir pushing against a moving object. Points opposite the motion.

The method: two directions, checked separately

  1. Add the vertical forces. Total up minus total down.Two arrows pointing the same way add together.
  2. Add the horizontal forces. Total right minus total left.
  3. Both totals are zero → balanced. Either total isn't zero → unbalanced.One balanced direction isn't enough. Both have to cancel.

Worked example

Two people push a box across the floor while friction pushes back.

DirectionAdd upTotal
Vertical80 N up − 80 N down0 N
Horizontal(30 + 20) N right − 50 N left0 N

Both zero → balanced. The box slides at a constant velocity, or stays put if it wasn't moving.

Trust the numbers, not the picture. Longer arrows mean bigger forces, but 48 N and 50 N look almost the same. Always add up the labeled values.

Watch-outs

  • Balanced means the object isn't moving.Balanced means the velocity isn't changing. The object could be at rest, or it could be moving at a steady speed in a straight line. An FBD alone can't tell you which.
  • A moving object needs a force arrow pointing the way it moves.There is no "force of motion." A puck sliding across ice has only Fg and FN on it. It keeps moving because of inertia, not because something pushes it.
  • The vertical forces cancel, so it's balanced.Check both directions. Most diagrams have balanced vertical forces; the horizontal forces are usually where the answer is.
  • One force by itself can be balanced.With only one force, there is nothing to cancel it. A ball in free fall has only Fg, so its forces are always unbalanced, even at the top of its flight.
  • The arrows look the same length, so they're equal.Read the labels. Small differences are easy to miss by eye, and any difference at all makes the forces unbalanced.
