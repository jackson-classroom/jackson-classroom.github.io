Forces come in pairs. Push on something and it pushes back.

Every force is one object pushing or pulling on another, and the second object always pushes or pulls back. Those two forces are an action-reaction pair: the same size, opposite directions, acting on different objects.

Newton's third law

Newton's third lawWhen one object pushes or pulls on a second object, the second object pushes or pulls back on the first with a force that is equal in size and opposite in direction.

A force is always one object acting on another, so every force comes from an interaction between two objects. You can't push on something without it pushing back on you. Lean on a wall and the wall pushes back on your hands. That push is the reason you don't fall over.

The two forces in an interaction are called an action-reaction pair. Every pair follows the same four rules.

same size

Equal in size

Always exactly equal, no matter how big, heavy, or fast either object is.

You push a wall with 40 N. The wall pushes you with 40 N.
opposite directions

Opposite in direction

If one force points right, its partner points left. If one points up, its partner points down.

The floor pushes up on your feet. Your feet push down on the floor.
same kind

The same kind of force

A contact push pairs with a contact push. A gravity pull pairs with a gravity pull.

Earth pulls the ball down by gravity. The ball pulls Earth up by gravity.
two objects

Act on different objects

One force acts on each object. They never show up on the same free-body diagram.

The table pushes on the book. The book pushes on the table.
For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. That's the short way to say the third law. One force in the pair is the action and the other is the reaction, but it doesn't matter which you call which. They happen at the same time: the reaction doesn't come after the action. The two forces start together and stop together.

Finding a force's partner

Every force has a partner, its reaction. To find it, write the force as a sentence that names both objects, then flip the sentence around.

  1. Say the force as "A pushes (or pulls) on B."The bat pushes the ball forward.Every force has two objects: one doing the pushing or pulling, and one being pushed or pulled.
  2. Swap the two objects.The ball pushes the bat ___.
  3. Flip the direction. Keep the size and the kind of force the same.The ball pushes the bat backward, just as hard.

Every force on our free-body diagrams has a partner

The forceIts partner

Same colour in both columns: a force and its partner are always the same kind of force.

If they're equal and opposite, why don't they cancel?

If every force has an equal and opposite partner, how does anything ever speed up? Because forces only cancel when they act on the same object. That's exactly what a free-body diagram checks: one object, and every force acting on it. The two forces in a pair always act on different objects, so only one of them is ever on a given free-body diagram.

You push a box across the floor with 50 N, and friction pushes back on it with 30 N. The box pushes back on your hands with 50 N too. Does that cancel your push?

Forces on the boxAdd upTotal
Vertical200 N up − 200 N down0 N
Horizontal50 N right − 30 N left20 N right

No. The box's 50 N push acts on you, so it goes on your free-body diagram, not the box's. The forces on the box are unbalanced, so the box speeds up.

Draw one object at a time. Before you add up forces, ask what each one acts on. If a force acts on something else, it doesn't belong on this diagram.

Same force, different results

The two forces in a pair are always the same size, but the two objects don't have to react the same way. Remember Newton's second law from page 2: a = Fnet / m. The same force on a bigger mass gives a smaller acceleration.

InteractionThe pairWhat you see
You drop a ballEarth pulls the ball down. The ball pulls Earth up just as hard.The ball speeds up toward the ground. Earth's mass is so enormous that its acceleration is far too small to ever notice.
A bug hits a windshieldThe windshield pushes the bug. The bug pushes the windshield just as hard.The bug's tiny mass gets a huge acceleration. The car barely slows down.
Two skaters push apartEach skater pushes the other with the same size force.They both roll away, and the lighter skater moves away faster.
A cannon firesThe cannon pushes the ball forward. The ball pushes the cannon backward just as hard.The light ball flies off fast. The heavy cannon only rolls back slowly.
Equal forces, not equal accelerations. If a question asks which object feels the bigger force in a collision, the answer is neither: the forces are equal. If it asks which object accelerates more, look at the masses.

How anything gets moving

You can't push yourself. To get moving, you push on something else, and its push back on you is what moves you. Every way of getting around works like this.

push the floor back

Walking

Your shoe pushes backward on the floor. The floor pushes forward on your shoe. That forward push is static friction (page 4).

On slick ice there's almost no friction, so you can't push back on the ground, and it can't push you forward.
push the road back

Driving

The tires push backward on the road. The road pushes forward on the tires. That's the "road pushes it forward" force on the car diagrams from pages 1 and 2.

Tires spinning on ice can't push back on the road, so the car doesn't go.
push the water back

Swimming

Your hands and feet push water backward. The water pushes you forward.

A rowboat works the same way: the oars push water back, and the water pushes the boat forward.
push the exhaust down

Rockets

The engine pushes hot gas out the bottom. The gas pushes the rocket up.

Rockets work in space, where there's no air. They push on their own exhaust, not on the air.
push the floor down

Jumping

Your legs push down hard on the floor. The floor pushes up on you just as hard, and that push launches you into the air.

Jump from a small boat to a dock and the boat drifts backward: you push it one way, and it pushes you the other.
Tug-of-war. The rope pulls on both teams equally hard, so pulling the rope "harder" can't win by itself. The winning team is the one that pushes harder on the ground with their feet, so the ground pushes back on them harder. That push from the ground is friction.

Watch-outs

  • The forces in a pair cancel, so nothing could ever move.Forces only cancel when they act on the same object. The two forces in a pair act on different objects, so they're never on the same free-body diagram.
  • The bigger or faster object pushes harder.A truck and a bug that collide push on each other with the same size force. What's different is the acceleration: the smaller mass gets the bigger one.
  • The reaction happens after the action.Both forces start and stop at the same instant. Neither one causes the other, and either one can be called the action.
  • Earth pulls on a falling ball, but the ball doesn't pull on Earth.It does, and just as hard. Earth's mass is so huge that its acceleration is far too small to notice.
  • Walls and floors just sit there. They don't push.Anything you push on pushes back. When you lean on a wall, the wall pushes back on your hands. That's why you don't fall over.
