Types of Chemical Reactions

Most reactions in AP Chemistry fall into three families: acid–base, oxidation–reduction, and precipitation. To sort one, ask what actually changes hands: a proton, electrons, or partners.

4.7.A

Three questions, three families

Don't sort a reaction by what it looks like. Sort it by what moves.

Acid–base

A proton (H+) moves

The acid gives up an H+, and the base takes it.

Oxidation–reduction

Electrons move

Oxidation numbers change. Electrons go from the species oxidized to the species reduced.

Precipitation

A solid forms from ions

Ions in solution find new partners, and one new pair is insoluble.

4.7.A

Acid–base: a proton changes hands

Acid–base reaction

Acid–base reactions involve the transfer of one or more protons (H+ ions) from one chemical species to another.

A hydrogen atom that has lost its only electron is just a proton, so “H+” and “proton” mean the same thing. In this unit, look for an acid such as HCl or HNO3 reacting with a hydroxide such as NaOH, or with water. Follow the H+: it starts on the acid and ends up on the base.

Molecular equation

Net ionic equation: the H+ from the acid joins the OH− from the base

4.7.A

Redox: electrons change hands

Oxidation–reduction (redox) reaction

Redox reactions involve the transfer of one or more electrons between chemical species, shown by changes in oxidation numbers. Electrons move from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced.

An oxidation number is the charge an atom would have if every bond were ionic. If an element's oxidation number goes up, it lost electrons and was oxidized. If it goes down, it gained electrons and was reduced. The numbers above each atom here show the change:

amber = oxidized (number goes up) · blue = reduced (number goes down)

Zinc goes from 0 to +2, so each zinc atom lost two electrons. Copper goes from +2 to 0, so each Cu2+ gained them. 4.9 covers the rules for assigning oxidation numbers.

Combustion is redox

Combustion

Combustion is an important kind of redox reaction in which a substance reacts with oxygen gas. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon produces carbon dioxide and water.

Carbon goes from −4 to +4 (oxidized), and oxygen goes from 0 to −2 (reduced). Compounds with C, H, and O, like ethanol, also burn completely to CO2 and H2O.

A quick clue

An element on its own, like Zn(s), O2(g), or Cl2(g), on either side of the equation almost always means redox. A free element has an oxidation number of 0, and once it's in a compound or an ion, it doesn't.

4.7.A

Precipitation: a solid forms from solution

Precipitation reaction

Precipitation reactions usually involve mixing ions in aqueous solution to produce an insoluble or sparingly soluble ionic compound, the precipitate.

The one solubility rule to knowAll sodium (Na+), potassium (K+), ammonium (NH4+), and nitrate (NO3−) salts are soluble in water.

Swap partners

  1. Write the ions in each solution. [[Pb(NO3)2]] gives Pb2+ and NO3−; [[KI]] gives K+ and I−.
  2. Swap partners. The two possible new compounds are PbI2 and KNO3. Use the charges to write each formula: Pb2+ needs two I−.
  3. Apply the rule. KNO3 has K+ and NO3−, so it stays dissolved. The yellow solid has to be PbI2.

Molecular equation

Net ionic equation (K+ and NO3− are spectators)

What the rule can and can't tell you. It only tells you what's definitely soluble. If every possible new compound contains Na+, K+, NH4+, or NO3−, no precipitate can form. Mixing KCl and NaNO3 just gives a solution of four ions. When you need to know that some other compound is insoluble, an AP question tells you, for example by saying that a precipitate forms.
Watch out

Look-alikes

These are the reactions AP questions use to catch students who classify by appearance.

An acid is there, but it's redox

Zinc goes from 0 to +2 and hydrogen from +1 to 0. Electrons move from Zn to H+, so this is redox, not acid–base.

OH− is there, but it's precipitation

No proton moves and no oxidation number changes. The ions just combine into an insoluble solid.

Something breaks apart, but it's not redox

Every oxidation number stays the same (Ca +2, C +4, O −2), so no electrons move. It isn't in any of the three families.

One element, two roles

Oxygen in H2O2 is −1. Some of it goes to −2 (reduced) and some to 0 (oxidized). Same element, both roles: still redox.
