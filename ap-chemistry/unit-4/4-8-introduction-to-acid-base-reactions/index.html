Introduction to Acid-Base Reactions

In a Brønsted–Lowry acid–base reaction, a proton (H+) moves from one particle to another. Tracking that proton lets you name the acid, the base, and the conjugate each one becomes.

4.8.A.1

Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases

4.8.A.1
Brønsted–Lowry acid and base

A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor. A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor.

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron. Take the electron away and all that's left is the proton, so H+ and “proton” mean the same thing. An acid–base reaction moves one H+ from the acid to the base:

HCl gives up its H+, so it's the acid. H2O takes the H+, so it's the base. H3O+ is hydronium, a water molecule carrying an extra proton.

Notice that nothing about HCl changes except that it lost an H+. That's the whole reaction: one proton, moved from one particle to another.

4.8.A.3

Conjugate acid–base pairs

4.8.A.3
Conjugate acid–base pair

When an acid or base ionizes in water, the conjugate acid–base pairs can be identified. The two members of a pair differ by exactly one H+.

Conjugate base

The acid, minus one H+

Remove one H and make the charge one more negative.

Conjugate acid

The base, plus one H+

Add one H and make the charge one more positive.

Every proton transfer has two pairs: the acid with its conjugate base, and the base with its conjugate acid. Each pair is one reactant and one product.

amber acid and its conjugate base · blue base and its conjugate acid

The names make sense if you read the reaction backward: NH4+ can give its proton back to OH−, so NH4+ really is an acid, and OH− really is a base.

The three mistakes that cost points

  • Forgetting the charge. The conjugate base of HSO4− is SO42−, not SO4−. Removing H+ removes a positive charge.
  • Going the wrong way. The conjugate base of HSO4− has fewer H's (SO42−). H2SO4 is its conjugate acid.
  • Pairing the two reactants. HCl and H2O aren't a conjugate pair. A pair is one reactant and the product it becomes.
4.8.A.2

Water: acid or base

4.8.A.2
Water in acid–base reactions

In aqueous solution, water plays an important role in many acid–base reactions. Its structure lets it accept a proton from some dissolved species and donate a proton to others.

Water as a base: it takes a proton from HNO3

Water as an acid: it gives a proton to NH3

Water as both: one molecule gives, the other takes

A species that can act as either an acid or a base is amphoteric. To be amphoteric, it needs an H it can give up and room to take on one more. Water qualifies, and so do ions like HCO3− and HSO4−.

Two ways to write it. H+(aq) and H3O+(aq) mean the same thing. A bare proton in water is always attached to a water molecule, so H3O+ is the more accurate picture. Unit 8 picks up from here with pH and the strength of acids and bases.
