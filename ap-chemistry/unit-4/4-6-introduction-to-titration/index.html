Introduction to Titration

A titration adds a solution of known concentration to an unknown one until the two have reacted exactly. At that point, the equivalence point, a little stoichiometry reveals the unknown concentration.

4.6.A.1

The setup

4.6.A.1
Titration

A titration determines the amount of an analyte in a solution. The titrant has a known concentration of a substance that reacts specifically and completely with the analyte.

Titrant, in the buret. Known concentration. You measure exactly how much you add.

Analyte, in the flask. A measured volume with an unknown concentration, the thing you’re solving for.

Indicator, in the flask. A few drops of a substance that changes color when the analyte is used up.

Reading a buret

  • The scale reads downward: 0.00 mL is at the top, so the reading goes up as liquid drains out.
  • Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, estimating to ±0.01 mL.
  • You almost never start at exactly 0.00 mL. The volume delivered is final reading − initial reading.
4.6.A.1

The equivalence point

4.6.A.1
Equivalence point and endpoint

The equivalence point is reached when the analyte has been totally consumed by the titrant. It’s often signaled by a change in a property, such as color. That observable event is the endpoint.

At the equivalence point, the moles of titrant added are exactly what the balanced equation needs for the moles of analyte present. That means equal moles only when the ratio is 1 : 1.

The two terms aren’t quite the same thing. The equivalence point is a stoichiometric fact; the endpoint is what you see. A well-chosen indicator changes color within a drop of the equivalence point, so the two are close enough to treat as the same. Phenolphthalein, used in the titrations on this page, is colorless until the first drop of excess NaOH turns the solution pink.

4.6.A

The calculation

It’s the 4.5 road map with molarity at both ends:

volume × M of titrant→ mol titrant→ × ratio → mol analyte→ ÷ volume → M of analyte

Worked example: a 1 : 1 ratio

25.00 mL of HCl is titrated with 0.1000 M NaOH: . The buret reads 0.45 mL at the start and 24.95 mL at the endpoint.

  • Volume delivered: 24.95 − 0.45 = 24.50 mL = 0.02450 L.
  • Moles NaOH: 0.1000 M × 0.02450 L = 2.450 × 10−3 mol.
  • Ratio 1 : 1, so moles HCl = 2.450 × 10−3 mol.
  • [HCl] = 2.450 × 10−3 mol / 0.02500 L = 0.09800 M.

Worked example: a 1 : 2 ratio

Titrations aren’t only acid–base. Here, 20.00 mL of CaCl2 is titrated with 0.1000 M AgNO3, and 31.40 mL is needed to precipitate all the chloride: .

0.03140 L× 0.1000 mol AgNO31 L× 1 mol CaCl22 mol AgNO3= 1.570 × 10−3 mol CaCl2

[CaCl2] = 1.570 × 10−3 mol / 0.02000 L = 0.07850 M.

The trap. “At the equivalence point, moles of titrant = moles of analyte” is only true for a 1 : 1 reaction. Each CaCl2 has two chloride ions, so it takes two AgNO3 for every CaCl2, and skipping the ratio doubles the answer.
Beyond acid–base

Other titrations

Any reaction that is fast, goes to completion, and has a visible endpoint can be used for a titration.

Precipitation

AgNO3 titrant finds the chloride in a sample, as in the CaCl2 example above. At the equivalence point, moles of Ag+ added equal moles of Cl−.

Oxidation–reduction

Permanganate is deep purple and Mn2+ is nearly colorless, so it’s its own indicator. The ratio is 1 MnO4− : 5 Fe2+; the equation is always given.
