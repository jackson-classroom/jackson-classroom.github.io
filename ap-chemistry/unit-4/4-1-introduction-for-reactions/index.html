4.1.A

It comes down to composition

Every change in matter is either physical or chemical. The difference isn’t how dramatic it looks. It’s whether the substance you end up with is the same substance you started with.

4.1.A.1
Physical change

A substance’s properties change, but its composition doesn’t. The most common physical changes are phase changes (melting, freezing, boiling, condensing, sublimation) and forming or separating mixtures (dissolving, filtering, distilling).

4.1.A.2
Chemical change

Substances are transformed into new substances, usually with different compositions. Atoms are rearranged into new combinations, so what’s left has different properties from what you started with.

Same starting material, two different changes

Both of these start with liquid water and both make bubbles of gas. Only one makes a new substance.

Physical · boiling

The bubbles are still water

The molecules move apart into the gas phase. Cool the gas and you get the same liquid back.

Chemical · electrolysis

The bubbles are new substances

The O–H bonds break and the atoms recombine. Hydrogen and oxygen have completely different properties from water.

So the question to ask is always the same: after the change, is it still the same substance? The observations in the next section are how you gather evidence to answer it.

4.1.A.2

Clues that a chemical change happened

You can’t watch atoms rearrange, so you look for signs that something with new properties has formed. The course framework names four kinds of evidence.

Heat or light

Magnesium burns with a blinding white light

The shiny metal becomes a white powder. Energy released as heat or light, or absorbed so the surroundings cool, is a sign that bonds were broken and formed.

Formation of a gas

Zinc fizzes in hydrochloric acid

Nothing is boiling. The bubbles are hydrogen, a gas that wasn’t there before.

Formation of a precipitate

Two colorless solutions make a yellow solid

A precipitate is an insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed. Here, PbI2 wasn’t present in either one.

Color change

Copper wire turns a colorless solution blue

The blue comes from Cu2+ ions that weren’t in solution before, and silver crystals grow on the wire.

Possible evidence, not proof. The framework calls these possible evidence for a reason: every one of them can also happen without a chemical change. A strong argument uses the clue to point to a specific new substance.
Watch out

When the evidence misleads

Each clue has a physical-change look-alike. These are the ones AP questions like to use.

Bubbles without a reaction

Boiling, or opening a soda

Bubbles in boiling water are H2O(g). Bubbles from a freshly opened soda are CO2 that was already dissolved and is coming back out of solution. Neither one is a new substance.

Temperature change without a reaction

An instant cold pack

Dissolving ammonium nitrate makes the pack cold. Dissolving forms a mixture, so it’s usually classified as physical. For ionic solids the case can be argued either way, which is exactly what Topic 4.4 takes up.

Color change without a reaction

Diluting a solution, or subliming iodine

Adding water to a blue CuSO4 solution makes it paler, but the Cu2+ ions are just more spread out. Heating dark gray solid iodine gives a violet vapor that’s still I2.

A solid without a reaction

Freezing, or crystals from a cooling solution

A liquid freezing, or crystals of a solute coming out of a saturated solution as it cools, gives you a solid that is the same substance as before.

And the opposite trap

Some chemical changes give you almost nothing to see. Mix hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide and the result is still a clear, colorless solution. The only clue is that it gets warm.

New water molecules form from H+ and OH−, and that’s a chemical change even without a gas, a precipitate, or a color. You’ll write the net ionic equation for this reaction in Topic 4.2.

Evidence suggests; composition decides. When a question asks you to justify a claim, name the observation and say what new substance it indicates. “It bubbled” isn’t enough. “A gas formed at room temperature, so the bubbles can’t be boiling, and the gas is a new substance” is.
