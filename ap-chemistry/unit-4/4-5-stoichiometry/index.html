Stoichiometry

The coefficients in a balanced equation are a recipe written in moles. Stoichiometry uses them to predict how much product forms and which reactant runs out first.

4.5.A.1–2

Coefficients are mole ratios

4.5.A.1
Conservation makes it possible

Because atoms are conserved in a chemical process, you can calculate the amount of product from a known amount of reactant, or the amount of reactant needed for a known amount of product.

4.5.A.2
What the coefficients mean

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the proportions of the substances involved. They’re used in calculations based on the mole.

Read three ways:

Read asN2H2NH3 Particles1 molecule3 molecules2 molecules Moles1 mol3 mol2 mol Grams28.02 g6.05 g34.07 g

The particle and mole rows follow the coefficients. The gram row doesn’t: it’s 28.02 : 6.05 : 34.07, not 1 : 3 : 2. Mass is conserved (28.02 + 6.05 = 34.07), but the ratio only works in moles.

Always go through moles. A mole ratio can connect moles of one substance to moles of another. It can never connect grams to grams directly.
4.5.A.2

The road map

Every stoichiometry problem crosses the same bridge: into moles of what you know, across the mole ratio, and out of moles of what you want.

grams of A →÷ molar mass of A moles of A →× mole ratio B / A moles of B →× molar mass of B grams of B

The moles don’t have to come from grams. These enter or leave at either “moles” box:

Particles

moles = particles ÷ 6.022 × 1023

Solutions

moles = M × V (in liters)

Gases

moles = PV / RT

Worked example: grams to grams

Iron is made by reducing iron(III) oxide with carbon monoxide: . What mass of iron can be made from 50.0 g of Fe2O3 with excess CO?

50.0 g Fe2O3× 1 mol Fe2O3159.70 g Fe2O3× 2 mol Fe1 mol Fe2O3× 55.85 g Fe1 mol Fe= 35.0 g Fe

“Excess CO” tells you CO won’t run out, so Fe2O3 decides how much iron forms.

4.5.A.1

Which reactant runs out first?

When neither reactant is in excess, one of them runs out first and stops the reaction. That’s the limiting reactant. Whatever is left of the other one is the excess reactant. You saw this in 4.3 with particles:

: six H2 need only three O2, so H2 runs out first even though there are more H2 molecules. One O2 is left over.

The method

  1. Convert every reactant to moles.
  2. Divide each by its coefficient. The smallest result is the limiting reactant. That number is how many “sets” of the reaction can happen.
  3. Calculate every product, and the amount of excess reactant used, from the limiting reactant only.
Compare moles ÷ coefficient, never grams. The reactant with the smaller mass, or even the smaller number of moles, isn’t automatically the limiting one.

Worked example with a BCA table

14.0 g of N2 and 6.05 g of H2 react: . Moles: N2 = 14.0 / 28.02 = 0.500 mol; H2 = 6.05 / 2.016 = 3.00 mol. Divide by coefficients: N2 0.500 / 1 = 0.500 and H2 3.00 / 3 = 1.00, so N2 is limiting.

A BCA table (Before, Change, After) keeps track of every substance in moles:

N2H2NH3
BEFORE0.5003.000
CHANGE−0.500−1.50+1.00
AFTER01.501.00

The Change row is in the ratio of the coefficients, 1 : 3 : 2, with reactants going down and products going up. The limiting reactant ends at zero. So 1.00 mol NH3 × 17.03 g/mol = 17.0 g NH3 forms, and 1.50 mol (3.02 g) of H2 is left over.

Gases and solutions

4.5.A.3
Combining with other relationships

Stoichiometry can be combined with the ideal gas law and with molarity to work quantitatively with gases and solutions.

Solutions: a precipitation from Topic 4.2

50.0 mL of 0.200 M BaCl2 is mixed with 40.0 mL of 0.300 M Na2SO4. What mass of BaSO4 precipitates?

  • Moles from M × V: Ba2+ = 0.200 × 0.0500 L = 0.0100 mol; SO42− = 0.300 × 0.0400 L = 0.0120 mol.
  • The net ionic equation gives the ratio: , 1 : 1. Ba2+ is limiting.
  • 0.0100 mol BaSO4 × 233.39 g/mol = 2.33 g BaSO4, with 0.0020 mol SO42− left in solution.

Gases: an airbag

An airbag inflates when sodium azide decomposes: . What mass of NaN3 produces 65.0 L of N2 at 1.00 atm and 25 °C?

n = PV/RT = (1.00 atm)(65.0 L)(0.08206 L·atm/mol·K)(298 K) = 2.66 mol N2
