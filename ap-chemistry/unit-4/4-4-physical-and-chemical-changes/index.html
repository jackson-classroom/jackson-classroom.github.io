Physical and Chemical Changes

At the particle level, the line between physical and chemical change comes down to what gets broken: chemical bonds or intermolecular attractions. Some processes, like dissolving a salt, can be argued either way.

4.4.A.1

Bonds or attractions?

4.4.A.1
Classifying by interactions

Processes that break and/or form chemical bonds are typically classified as chemical processes. Processes that change only intermolecular interactions, such as phase changes, are typically classified as physical processes.

Chemical bonds

Hold atoms together within a particle

Covalent bonds inside molecules (the O–H bonds in water), ionic bonds holding a lattice of ions together, and metallic bonds. Break them and rearrange the atoms, and you have new substances.

Intermolecular forces

Attractions between separate particles

Hydrogen bonding, dipole–dipole forces, and London dispersion forces. Overcome them and the particles move apart, but each particle is unchanged.

Same water, two different processes

Boiling, : the hydrogen bonds between molecules are overcome. Every H2O molecule is still intact.

Electrolysis, : the O–H bonds inside the molecules break, and new H–H and O=O bonds form.

4.4.A

How much energy each one takes

Intermolecular forces are much weaker than the chemical bonds inside molecules. For one mole of water:

Breaking the bonds inside water molecules takes more than 20 times the energy of boiling the water. That’s why water boils at 100 °C, but decomposing water into H2 and O2 just by heating it takes temperatures above 2000 °C.

Macroscopic clue. A process that happens at a modest temperature, like melting or boiling a molecular substance, is usually overcoming intermolecular forces. A large energy change, or a substance with new properties, points to bonds being broken and formed.

4.4.A.2

When a physical process breaks bonds

4.4.A.2
Dissolving a salt

Sometimes physical processes involve breaking chemical bonds. Dissolving a salt in water breaks ionic bonds and forms ion–dipole interactions between the ions and water, so plausible arguments can be made for calling it either a physical or a chemical process.

When NaCl dissolves, each ion leaves the lattice and is surrounded by water molecules. The partly negative oxygen ends face Na+, and the partly positive hydrogen ends face Cl−.

The case for physical
  • No new substance forms. The Na+ and Cl− ions in solution are the same ions that made up the solid.
  • Evaporate the water and you get NaCl back.
  • Dissolving forms a mixture, which Topic 4.1 calls a physical change.
The case for chemical
  • The ionic bonds between Na+ and Cl− in the lattice are broken.
  • New ion–dipole interactions form between the ions and water molecules.
  • Properties change: the solution conducts electricity, and solid NaCl doesn’t.
On the AP Exam, the course framework treats both claims as plausible. What earns the point is the reasoning: name the interactions that are broken (ionic bonds in the lattice) and the ones that form (ion–dipole interactions with water).

Other physical processes that break bonds

Melting an ionic solid or a covalent network solid is a phase change, so it’s typically called physical, but it breaks bonds. NaCl melts at 801 °C because the ionic bonds holding the lattice together must be broken. Quartz, SiO2, melts at about 1700 °C because covalent Si–O bonds must be broken. Melting points that high are a clue that bonds, not just intermolecular forces, are involved.
