Net Ionic Equations

When ionic compounds react in water, most of the ions never change. A net ionic equation strips those spectators away and shows only the particles that actually react.

Learning objective 4.2.A Represent changes in matter with a balanced chemical or net ionic equation: (a) for physical changes, (b) for given information about the identity of the reactants and/or product, (c) for ions in a given chemical reaction.

4.2.A.1–2

Every change gets a balanced equation

4.2.A.1
Physical and chemical processes

Every physical and chemical process can be represented with a balanced equation, not just reactions.

4.2.A.2
Conservation of atoms

A chemical change rearranges atoms into new combinations, so a balanced equation has the same number of atoms of every element on both sides. That’s why mass is conserved in a reaction.

Physical changes have equations too

Part (a) of the learning objective asks you to write equations for physical changes. The formula doesn’t change; the state does. Watch what happens when a solid dissolves, though: molecular and ionic solids are written differently.

Phase change

Melting ice, subliming dry ice. Same substance on both sides.

Dissolving

Glucose is molecular and dissolves as intact molecules. Calcium chloride is ionic and separates into its ions.

Charge balances too. Once ions appear in an equation, atoms aren’t the only thing that has to balance. The total charge on the left must equal the total charge on the right. In the CaCl2 equation above: 0 on the left, (+2) + 2(−1) = 0 on the right.

4.2.A.3
Different forms, same reaction

Balanced molecular, complete ionic, and net ionic equations are different symbolic forms of the same chemical reaction. Which form you use depends on the context.

Molecular

What you’d actually measure out

Every compound as a whole formula. Use it for stoichiometry with real reagents: you can weigh out KBr, but you can’t weigh out Br− on its own.

Complete ionic

Everything that’s in the beaker

Every dissolved ionic compound written as its ions. It’s the bookkeeping step that lets you see which ions never change.

4.2.A(c)

Split it, or keep it together?

This is the decision that makes or breaks a net ionic equation. Write a substance as separate ions only if it’s an ionic compound that is dissolved. In solution its ions have separated and move around independently. Everything else keeps its formula.

Write as ions

Dissolved ionic compounds (aq)

  • Subscripts become coefficients. The 2 after (NO3) means two separate nitrate ions.
  • Polyatomic ions stay whole. NO3−, SO42−, PO43−, NH4+, and OH− never come apart.
  • Charges come from the formula. FeCl3 has three Cl−, so the iron is Fe3+.
Keep as one formula

Everything else

  • Solids: precipitates like AgCl(s), and metals like Cu(s). An ionic solid’s ions are locked in place, so it isn’t split, even though it’s ionic.
  • Liquids and gases: H2O(l), O2(g).
  • Molecular compounds, even when dissolved: C6H12O6(aq), CH3OH(aq). They dissolve as whole molecules, not ions.
Solubility on the AP Exam. You’re expected to know that all sodium, potassium, ammonium, and nitrate salts are soluble in water (the course framework states this in Topic 4.7). For any other compound, the question gives you what you need, usually the state symbol or the fact that a precipitate forms.

Acids and bases come with their own rules for what gets split, and those come in Unit 8. Every reaction on this page is a precipitation or a metal reacting with a metal ion.

4.2.A(c)

Cancel the spectators

A spectator ion is dissolved before the reaction and still dissolved, unchanged, after it. It appears on both sides of the complete ionic equation in the same amount, so it cancels.

  1. Write the balanced molecular equation with a state on every species.
  2. Split every dissolved ionic compound. The coefficient multiplies through: becomes .
  3. Cross out spectators: the same ion, with the same charge and the same coefficient, on both sides.
  4. Reduce the coefficients if they all share a common factor.
  5. Check that atoms balance and charge balances.

No reaction, no net ionic equation. Mix NaCl(aq) with KNO3(aq) and every ion is a spectator: all four possible products are soluble, so nothing leaves the solution. Cross everything out and nothing is left. That’s no reaction.
Spectators don’t disappear. They’re left out of the net ionic equation because they don’t change, not because they’re gone. After the reaction above, the Ba2+ and NO3− ions are still dissolved in the beaker.
