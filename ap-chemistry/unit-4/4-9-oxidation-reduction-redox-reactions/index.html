Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Reactions

In a redox reaction, electrons move from one species to another. Oxidation numbers show where they went, and half-reactions show each side of the trade separately.

Skill 1

Assigning oxidation numbers

An oxidation number is the charge an atom would have if every bond were ionic, with the shared electrons all given to the more electronegative atom. For a monatomic ion it's the real charge. For an atom in a molecule or a polyatomic ion, it's bookkeeping that lets you track electrons.

Oxidation numbers are written sign first, like +3 or −2, so they don't get mixed up with ion charges (3+, 2−).

The rules

When two rules disagree, the one higher on the list wins.

#RuleExamples
1An element by itself is 0.Fe, O2, Cl2, S8
2A monatomic ion is its charge.Fe3+ is +3; S2− is −2
3The oxidation numbers add up to the overall charge: 0 for a neutral compound, the ion's charge for a polyatomic ion.In SO42−, they total −2
4F is always −1. In compounds, group 1 metals are +1, group 2 metals are +2, and Al is +3.Na in NaCl is +1; Ca in CaCO3 is +2
5H is +1, except −1 when bonded to a metal.+1 in H2O; −1 in NaH
6O is −2, except −1 in peroxides.−2 in H2O; −1 in H2O2
7Cl, Br, and I are −1, except when bonded to O.−1 in NaCl; +5 in ClO3−

The method: use the rules for every atom you can, then solve for the one that's left so the total comes out right.

A polyatomic ion

S in

O is −2: 4 × (−2) = −8
Total must be −2
x + (−8) = −2 → x = +6

A compound

Cr in

K is +1: 2 × (+1) = +2
O is −2: 7 × (−2) = −14
2x + 2 − 14 = 0 → x = +6

An exception

O in

H is +1 (rule 5 beats rule 6): 2 × (+1) = +2
2x + 2 = 0 → x = −1 (a peroxide)

Two of the same element

C in

O is −2: 4 × (−2) = −8
2x − 8 = −2 → x = +3
Each C is +3, not the total.

Skill 2

Oxidized or reduced?

Oxidation

Loses electrons: the number goes up

Zn → Zn2+: 0 to +2. Losing two negative electrons makes the atom two units more positive.

Reduction

Gains electrons: the number goes down

Cu2+ → Cu: +2 to 0. The oxidation number is literally reduced.

One way to remember it: OIL RIG, oxidation is loss, reduction is gain (of electrons). The two always happen together. Electrons move from the species that is oxidized to the species that is reduced, so every electron lost is gained by something else.

Reading a reaction

Assign an oxidation number to every atom, then look for the ones that change.

amber = oxidized (number goes up) · blue = reduced (number goes down)

Iron goes from +3 to 0, so iron is reduced. Carbon goes from +2 to +4, so carbon is oxidized. Oxygen is −2 everywhere, so it isn't involved in the electron transfer at all.

This is the titration reaction from 4.6. Manganese goes from +7 to +2 (reduced) and iron from +2 to +3 (oxidized). H and O don't change.

Element or species? Read what the question asks. “Which element is oxidized?” wants iron. “Which species is oxidized?” wants the particle that contains it: Fe2+. You may also see the terms oxidizing agent (the species that gets reduced, MnO4− here) and reducing agent (the species that gets oxidized, Fe2+ here).

4.9.A.1
Half-reactions

Balanced chemical equations for redox reactions can be constructed from half-reactions.

A half-reaction shows one side of the electron trade on its own, with the electrons written in. The electrons tell you which half it is:

Oxidation: electrons are a product (they're lost)

Reduction: electrons are a reactant (they're gained)

A balanced half-reaction balances atoms and charge. In the oxidation above, the left side has a charge of 0, and the right side has +2 + 2(−1) = 0.

Writing a simple half-reaction

For a metal, a monatomic ion, or a diatomic element, the number of electrons is the change in oxidation number times the number of atoms that change:

  • : one Al, 0 → +3, so 3 electrons.
  • : two Br, each −1 → 0, so 2 electrons.
  • : two Cl, each 0 → −1, so 2 electrons gained.
In our class: when a half-reaction includes H+, OH−, or H2O, like the permanganate one below, it will be given to you already balanced. You won't have to balance half-reactions in acidic or basic solution from scratch. You do need to write simple ones like those above, and combine any pair you're given.
Skill 3

Combining half-reactions

Electrons can't pile up anywhere: every electron the oxidation releases, the reduction has to take. So the half-reactions have to be scaled until the electrons match.

  1. Match the electrons. Multiply each half-reaction by the smallest whole number that makes electrons lost equal electrons gained.
  2. Add the half-reactions. Everything on the left goes on the left, everything on the right on the right.
  3. Cancel. The electrons must cancel completely. Also cancel anything else that shows up on both sides, usually H+ or H2O.
  4. Check. Atoms balance, and the total charge is the same on both sides.

Worked example: different electron counts

Aluminum metal in a solution of Cu2+. Aluminum gives 3 electrons and copper takes 2, so both are scaled to 6.

Worked example: given half-reactions with H+ and H2O

The 4.6 titration. The permanganate half-reaction is given balanced; your job is only to combine.

Worked example: something else cancels

Hydrogen peroxide reacting with permanganate. After adding, H+ appears on both sides, so 10 of them cancel.
