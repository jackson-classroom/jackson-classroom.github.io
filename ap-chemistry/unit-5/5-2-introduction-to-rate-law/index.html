Introduction to Rate Law

A rate law tells you exactly how the rate depends on each reactant’s concentration. The exponents, called reaction orders, have to come from experiment, not from the balanced equation.

5.2.A.1–2

What does a rate law say?

Topic 5.1 said that a higher reactant concentration makes a reaction faster. A rate law says how much faster.

5.2.A.2
Rate law

The rate law expresses the rate of a reaction as proportional to the concentration of each reactant raised to a power.

For a reaction with reactants A and B:

rate = k [A]m [B]n
rate

How fast the reaction is going, in M/s.

[A] and [B]

The concentrations of the reactants, in M.

m and n

The orders. They say how strongly each concentration affects the rate.

k

The rate constant. One number for this reaction at this temperature.

The orders aren’t the coefficients. You can’t read m and n off the balanced equation. They have to be measured. For the coefficient is 2, but experiments show rate = k[N2O5], with an exponent of 1.
5.2.A.1
Where the data come from

Experimental methods can be used to monitor the amounts of reactants or products of a reaction and to determine its rate.

Those are the methods from Topic 5.1: collect a gas, follow a mass or a pressure, or measure absorbance as a color appears or fades. Every rate law starts as a table of measured rates.

5.2.A.3

What an order means

5.2.A.3
Order

The power of each reactant in the rate law is the order of the reaction with respect to that reactant. The sum of the powers is the overall order of the reaction.

The order tells you what happens to the rate when you change that one concentration and keep everything else the same. If the concentration is multiplied by some factor, the rate is multiplied by that factor raised to the order.

Order in ARate lawDouble [A]Triple [A]Halve [A]
Zerorate = kno change (20 = 1)no changeno change
Firstrate = k[A]rate × 2rate × 3rate × ½
Secondrate = k[A]2rate × 4rate × 9rate × ¼
  • An exponent of 1 isn’t written: [A]1 is just [A].
  • A zero-order reactant is left out of the rate law, because [A]0 = 1. It still has to be there for the reaction to happen. Its concentration just doesn’t change the rate.
  • In AP Chemistry the orders you’ll find are 0, 1, and 2.

Overall order

Add the exponents. For rate = k[NO]2[H2], the reaction is second order in NO, first order in H2, and 2 + 1 = third order overall.

5.2.A.5

Finding the orders from initial rates

5.2.A.5
Method of initial rates

Comparing initial rates of a reaction is a method to determine the order with respect to each reactant.

Run the reaction several times. Each time, start with different concentrations and measure the rate right at the beginning, when you know the concentrations exactly. Then compare two trials in which only one concentration changed. Whatever happened to the rate was caused by that reactant.

Worked example

For at a fixed temperature:

Trial[NO] (M)[H2] (M)Initial rate (M/s)
10.100.101.2 × 10−3
20.100.202.4 × 10−3
30.200.104.8 × 10−3
  1. Order in H2: compare trials 1 and 2. [NO] stays at 0.10 M. [H2] doubles and the rate doubles. 2n = 2, so n = 1.
  2. Order in NO: compare trials 1 and 3. [H2] stays at 0.10 M. [NO] doubles and the rate goes up four times. 2m = 4, so m = 2.
  3. Write the rate law: rate = k[NO]2[H2]. Notice that the coefficient of H2 is 2 but its order is 1.

The same comparison written as a ratio, which is the form to show on a free-response question:

rate3rate1= k(0.20)m(0.10)nk(0.10)m(0.10)n= 4.8 × 10−31.2 × 10−3 so 2m = 4 and m = 2
Change one thing at a time. If both concentrations differ between two trials, you can’t tell which one changed the rate. Find one order from a clean pair first. Then you can use it to untangle a pair where both changed.
5.2.A.4

What is k?

5.2.A.4
Rate constant

The proportionality constant in the rate law is called the rate constant. Its value depends on temperature, and its units reflect the overall reaction order.

Calculating k

Once you know the orders, put the numbers from any one trial into the rate law and solve for k. With trial 1 above:

k = rate[NO]2[H2]= 1.2 × 10−3 M/s(0.10 M)2(0.10 M) = 1.2 M−2 s−1

Every trial gives the same k. That’s a good way to check your orders: if two trials give different values, an order is wrong.

With k in hand, the rate law predicts the rate for any concentrations at that temperature. If [NO] = 0.30 M and [H2] = 0.050 M, rate = (1.2)(0.30)2(0.050) = 5.4 × 10−3 M/s.

The units of k

The rate is always in M/s, so the units of k have to cancel whatever the concentrations leave behind. That makes them different for each overall order.

Overall orderExample rate lawUnits of kAlso written
Zerorate = kM s−1mol L−1 s−1
Firstrate = k[A]s−11/s
Secondrate = k[A][B]M−1 s−1L mol−1 s−1
Thirdrate = k[A]2[B]M−2 s−1L2 mol−2 s−1

The pattern: the power on M is 1 minus the overall order. On the exam, the units of k are a quick way to tell the overall order, and leaving them off a calculated k can cost a point.

What changes k, and what doesn’t

Changes k

Temperature and catalysts

Raising the temperature makes k larger, which is how warming a reaction speeds it up. Adding a catalyst also gives a larger k.

Doesn’t change k

Concentration

Changing a concentration changes the rate, but k stays the same. So does k as the reaction runs and the reactants are used up.
