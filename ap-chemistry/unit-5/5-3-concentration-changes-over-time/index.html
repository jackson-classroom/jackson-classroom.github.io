5.3.A.1

What does concentration do over time?

Topic 5.2 found a rate law by running several trials and comparing their initial rates. There’s a second way. Run the reaction once, and measure the concentration of a reactant again and again as it gets used up.

5.3.A.1
Order from a graph

The order of a reaction can be inferred from a graph of concentration of reactant versus time.

As the reaction runs, [A] falls. What that does to the rate depends on the order, and that sets the shape of the graph.

Three reactions that start at the same concentration and the same rate.

Zero order

rate = k

The rate doesn’t depend on [A], so it never slows down. [A] falls along a straight line until it runs out.

First order

rate = k[A]

When half the A is gone, the rate is half as large. The graph is a curve that keeps flattening.

Second order

rate = k[A]2

When half the A is gone, the rate is only a quarter as large. The curve flattens sooner and has a long tail.

Zero order is the unusual one. It shows up when something other than concentration limits the rate, such as a reaction that happens on a metal surface that’s already completely covered with reactant.

Two curves look a lot alike. You can spot zero order by eye, because its graph is straight. But first- and second-order curves are hard to tell apart just by looking. The fix is to change what goes on the y-axis until the data fall on a straight line.
5.3.A.2–3

Which graph is a straight line?

Each order has its own integrated rate law, an equation that gives [A] at any time t. Each one can be written in the form y = mx + b, with time on the x-axis. Only the y-axis is different.

5.3.A.2
First order

If a reaction is first order with respect to a reactant being monitored, a plot of the natural log (ln) of the reactant concentration as a function of time will be linear.

5.3.A.3
Second order

If a reaction is second order with respect to a reactant being monitored, a plot of the reciprocal of the concentration of that reactant versus time will be linear.

[A]0 is the concentration at the start and [A]t is the concentration at time t. All three equations are on the AP equation sheet, so you don’t have to memorize them. You do need to know which one goes with which order.

To see the straight line, move the starting value to the other side. For first order:

ln[A]t = −k · t + ln[A]0 matches y = m · x + b

So y is ln[A], x is time, the slope is −k, and the y-intercept is ln[A]0.

The test

  1. From the data, make three graphs: [A] vs. time, ln[A] vs. time, and 1/[A] vs. time.
  2. See which one is a straight line. Only one will be.
  3. That graph tells you the order: [A] is zero, ln[A] is first, 1/[A] is second.
Without graphing: if the times are evenly spaced, a straight line means the y-value changes by the same amount in every interval. Subtract down each column of a data table and look for the column with a constant difference.
5.3.A.4

What does the slope tell me?

5.3.A.4
Rate constant from the slope

The slopes of the concentration versus time data for zeroth-, first-, and second-order reactions can be used to determine the rate constant for the reaction.

Once you’ve found the straight line, its slope gives you k. For zero and first order the line slopes down, and k = −slope. For second order the line slopes up, and k = slope. Either way, k is a positive number.

Worked example

The decomposition from Topic 5.1 was followed for 800 seconds at constant temperature.

  2. Check 1/[A]. It rises by 20.2, then 28.5, then 40.0, then 56.1. The steps get bigger, so 1/[A] vs. time is a curve. Not second order.
  3. Check ln[A]. It drops by 0.340 every time. A constant change means ln[A] vs. time is a straight line, so the reaction is first order: rate = k[N2O5].

The five ln[N2O5] values fall on one straight line. Its slope is −k.

The units of k match the order, just as in Topic 5.2: M s−1 for zero order, s−1 for first, and M−1 s−1 for second. They’re also the units of each graph’s slope.

Why the coefficient 2 didn’t matter. The order came from the data, not from the balanced equation. The equation has 2N2O5, but the straight ln plot shows the reaction is first order.
5.3.A.4

Using the equations

Each integrated rate law connects four things: [A]0, [A]t, k, and t. Know any three and you can find the fourth. Pick the equation that matches the order.

How much is left? (first order)

For the N2O5 reaction above, k = 1.70 × 10−3 s−1 and [N2O5]0 = 0.0200 M. What is the concentration after 600 s?

ln[A]t = ln[A]0 − kt = ln(0.0200) − (1.70 × 10−3 s−1)(600 s) = −3.912 − 1.020 = −4.932
[A]t = e−4.932 = 0.00721 M

That matches the data table. The last step undoes the natural log with ex on your calculator.

How long does it take? (second order)

The decomposition is second order in NO2, with k = 0.54 M−1 s−1 at 300 °C. How long does it take [NO2] to fall from 0.0100 M to 0.0050 M?

1[A]t− 1[A]0= kt → 10.0050 M− 10.0100 M = 200 − 100 = 100 M−1
t = 100 M−10.54 M−1 s−1 = 1.9 × 102 s (185 s before rounding)
Signs to watch. The zero- and first-order equations have −kt, because [A] and ln[A] go down. The second-order equation has +kt, because 1/[A] goes up as [A] goes down. If you get a negative time or a negative k, a subtraction is the wrong way round.
5.3.A.5–6

How long until half is gone?

The half-life, t½, is the time it takes for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half of what it was.

5.3.A.5
Half-life of a first-order reaction

Half-life is a critical parameter for first-order reactions because the half-life is constant and related to the rate constant for the reaction by the equation t½ = 0.693/k.

The equation comes from the first-order integrated rate law. At one half-life, [A]t is half of [A]0:

ln [A]t[A]0 = ln(½) = −0.693 = −k t½ so t½ = 0.693k

Notice what isn’t in that equation: the concentration. For a first-order reaction it takes the same time to go from 1.0 M to 0.50 M as from 0.50 M to 0.25 M, or from any amount to half of it.

For the N2O5 reaction, t½ = 0.693 / (1.70 × 10−3 s−1) = 408 s. Check it against the data table: at 400 s the concentration is 0.0101 M, about half of 0.0200 M, and at 800 s it’s 0.00513 M, about a quarter.

Radioactive decay

5.3.A.6
Radioactive decay

Radioactive decay processes provide an important illustration of first-order kinetics.

Every radioactive isotope decays with first-order kinetics, so each one has a fixed half-life. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years, which is what makes carbon dating work.

Example. Iodine-131, used to treat thyroid disease, has a half-life of 8.0 days. How much of a 40. mg sample is left after 24 days?

  • 24 days ÷ 8.0 days = 3 half-lives.
  • 40. mg → 20. mg → 10. mg → 5.0 mg, which is 40. mg × (½)3.
  • If you need k: k = 0.693 / 8.0 days = 0.087 day−1.
First order

Constant half-life

Every half-life takes the same time, whatever the concentration. If the data show a constant half-life, the reaction is first order.

Zero and second order

Half-life changes

For zero order, each half-life is shorter than the last. For second order, each one is longer. t½ = 0.693/k works only for first order.
