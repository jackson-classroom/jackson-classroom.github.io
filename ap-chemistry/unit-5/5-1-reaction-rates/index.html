Reaction Rates

Some reactions are over in a flash and others take years. Reaction rate measures how fast reactants are used up or products appear, and it depends on concentration, temperature, surface area, and catalysts.

5.1.A.1

How fast is it going?

5.1.A.1
Kinetics

The kinetics of a chemical reaction is the rate at which an amount of reactants is converted to products per unit of time.

A car’s speed is a change in distance divided by a change in time. A reaction’s rate is a change in concentration divided by a change in time, so its usual unit is M/s (the same thing as mol/(L·s) or mol L−1 s−1).

rate of change of A = Δ[A]Δt = [A]final − [A]initialtfinal − tinitial

The square brackets mean “concentration of, in mol/L.” A reactant’s concentration goes down, so its Δ[A]/Δt is negative. A product’s concentration goes up, so its Δ[A]/Δt is positive. A rate of disappearance or appearance is reported as a positive number, because the word already tells you the direction.

N2O5 disappears at an average rate of 3.1 × 10−5 M/s at first, but only 1.9 × 10−5 M/s later on. The reaction slows down as the reactant is used up. That’s true of most reactions, and the reason comes up below: less reactant means a lower concentration.

Average rate and instantaneous rate

Because the rate keeps changing, there are two ways to state it. Both are slopes on a graph of concentration against time.

Average rate

Slope between two points

Δ[A]/Δt over a whole time interval. It’s the slope of the straight line (a secant) joining two points on the curve.

Instantaneous rate

Slope at one point

The rate at a single moment. It’s the slope of the tangent line that just touches the curve there. The initial rate is the instantaneous rate at t = 0, where the curve is steepest.

Steeper means faster. Whether the curve goes up or down doesn’t matter. A flat curve means the concentration has stopped changing.

How a rate is measured in the lab

You can’t count molecules, so you follow something that changes along with concentration and record it over time.

A gas is produced

Collect the gas and record its volume, track the pressure in a sealed flask, or leave the flask on a balance and record the mass lost.

A color changes

Measure absorbance with a spectrophotometer. By the Beer-Lambert law, absorbance is proportional to the concentration of the colored species.

5.1.A.2

How do the rates of different species compare?

5.1.A.2
Stoichiometry sets the ratio

The rates of change of reactant and product concentrations are determined by the stoichiometry in the balanced chemical equation.

In , every 2 N2O5 that react make 4 NO2 and 1 O2. So in any stretch of time, NO2 appears twice as fast as N2O5 disappears, and O2 appears half as fast. It’s the mole ratio from Topic 4.5, applied to rates.

Worked example: from one rate to another

In the first 100 s above, N2O5 disappears at 3.1 × 10−5 M/s. How fast do NO2 and O2 appear?

3.1 × 10−5 M/s N2O5× 4 mol NO22 mol N2O5 = 6.2 × 10−5 M/s NO2
3.1 × 10−5 M/s N2O5× 1 mol O22 mol N2O5 = 1.6 × 10−5 M/s O2

One rate for the whole reaction

Three species, three different numbers. To give the reaction a single rate that doesn’t depend on which species you watched, divide each rate of change by that species’ coefficient, and put a minus sign on the reactants so the result is positive.

rate = −12Δ[N2O5]Δt = 14Δ[NO2]Δt = Δ[O2]Δt

Here all three give 1.6 × 10−5 M/s. For a general reaction aA + bB → cC + dD:

rate = −1aΔ[A]Δt = −1bΔ[B]Δt = 1cΔ[C]Δt = 1dΔ[D]Δt
Bigger coefficient, faster change. The species with the largest coefficient has the steepest curve. A common mistake is to flip the ratio: check that your answer is larger for the species with the larger coefficient.
5.1.A.3

How do I speed it up?

5.1.A.3
What rate depends on

The rate of a reaction is influenced by reactant concentrations, temperature, surface area, catalysts, and other environmental factors.

Particles have to collide before they can react. Each factor below works by changing how often reactant particles collide, or how many of those collisions succeed. Topic 5.5 builds that idea into a full model. For now, know the four factors and which way each one pushes the rate.

Concentration

More particles in the same space

A higher reactant concentration means more collisions every second, so the rate goes up. For gases, squeezing the same amount into a smaller volume raises the pressure and the concentration together.

Temperature

Faster particles, harder collisions

Warmer particles collide a little more often, and far more of the collisions have enough energy to react. Raising the temperature speeds up nearly every reaction.

Surface area

More of the solid exposed

A solid can only react at its surface. Crushing a lump into powder exposes more particles to the other reactant. This matters when the reactants are in different phases, such as a solid in a solution.

Catalyst

An easier path

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up, so it’s still there at the end. It doesn’t appear as a reactant or product in the overall equation. Topic 5.11 explains how.

The identity of the reactants matters too. Two dissolved ions meeting to form a precipitate react almost instantly, while a reaction that has to break strong covalent bonds can be very slow. Some reactions also speed up in light.

Faster isn’t more. These factors change how quickly the products form, not how much forms in the end. The amount of product is still set by the limiting reactant. Powdered zinc and a zinc strip of the same mass make the same amount of H2 in excess acid. The powder just gets there sooner.

This also explains the slowing you saw in the data. As a reaction runs, its reactants are used up, their concentrations fall, and the rate falls with them.
