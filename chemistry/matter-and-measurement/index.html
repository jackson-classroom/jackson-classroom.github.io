Reading the instrument

Every measurement ends in a guess.

Not a wild guess — a disciplined one. When you write down a measurement, every digit but the last is certain. The last digit is your best estimate, and leaving it off is just as wrong as making one up.

The one-estimated-digit rule

An instrument only takes you so far. A ruler marked in millimeters tells you, with certainty, that your cube is somewhere between 2.4 cm and 2.5 cm. It cannot tell you where in that gap. So you look, you judge, and you commit to a digit: 2.43 cm.

That last digit is uncertain, and everyone reading your data knows it. That's the point. It's how a number carries its own honesty.

How far do you estimate?

  • Analog tools (rulers, graduated cylinders, triple beam balances) — find the smallest marked division, then estimate one place beyond it. Marks every 1 mL, so you report tenths.
  • Digital tools (electronic balances) — write down every digit the display gives you, including trailing zeros. The instrument already did the estimating; the last digit shown is the uncertain one.
The trap: if the meniscus sits exactly on the 44 mL line, you do not write 44 mL. You write 44.0 mL. That zero is a real measurement — it says "I looked, and it's not 43.9 or 44.1." Dropping it throws away information you actually collected.
Cylinders & beakers

Reading glassware

Water in glass pulls itself into a curve called the meniscus. Read it at eye level, from the bottom of the curve. Looking down at it from above will cost you a millimeter or two every time.

Rulers

Reading a ruler

Your metric ruler is marked every millimeter — that's every 0.1 cm. So you can be certain to the tenth of a centimeter and you estimate the hundredth. Measurements in centimeters coming off this ruler have two decimal places.

Balances

Three balances, one object

You massed the same object on three different balances and got three different numbers of digits. None of them is lying. Each one is reporting the best it can do, and the number of decimal places is the claim about precision.

Place the object on each balance

The analytical balance reports four decimal places, so it claims certainty down to the tenth of a milligram. The triple beam claims only hundredths of a gram. Recording 12.35 g from the analytical balance would be throwing away two digits you paid for — and recording 12.3500 g from the triple beam would be inventing two you never had.

Counting significant digits

The significant digits of a measurement are the ones that carry real information: all the certain digits plus that one estimated digit. Zeros are the only complication, and only because they do double duty — sometimes they're measurements, sometimes they're just placeholders holding the decimal point in position.

  • Leading zeros never count. 0.0042 g has 2 significant digits. Writing it as 4.2 mg doesn't change the precision, so those zeros can't be carrying any.
  • Zeros between nonzero digits always count. 1.04 cm has 3.
  • Trailing zeros count when there's a decimal point. 2.500 g has 4 — you wouldn't have written those zeros unless you'd measured them.
  • Trailing zeros with no decimal point are ambiguous. 1200 m could be 2, 3, or 4 — the writing simply doesn't say. Read it as 2 unless you're told otherwise. (Later this year you'll learn a notation that removes the guesswork.)

Precision vs. accuracy

Choosing your tool

Try this: have four people each pour "50 mL" from a beaker, then check each pour in a graduated cylinder. The readings won't be 50 mL, and they won't match each other. That isn't carelessness. A beaker marked every 25 or 50 mL simply cannot deliver a measurement with two decimal places, no matter how careful you are.

Two different words for two different problems:

  • Precision — how finely you can measure, and how closely repeated measurements agree. A tool's smallest division sets your ceiling.
  • Accuracy — how close you land to the true value. You can be precise and wrong: an analytical balance that was never zeroed will hand you four beautiful decimal places of nonsense.

The same idea applies to you as an instrument. Estimating 20 seconds with your eyes closed is a measurement with an uncertainty of several seconds. A stopwatch reading 17.62 s is precise to hundredths; your internal sense of time is not even close. Percent error is how you put a number on that gap.
