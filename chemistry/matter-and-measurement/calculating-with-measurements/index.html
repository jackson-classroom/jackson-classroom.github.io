Doing math with measurements

Your calculator does not know how carefully you measured.

It will happily hand you ten digits from two measurements that only justified two. Your job is to decide where the real answer stops — and then cut.

3.42 cm × 2.1 cm → the calculator says
7.182 cm²
2.1 cm had only two significant digits, so the answer stops after two. Then the discarded 8 rounds the 1 up: 7.2 cm². Everything past the cut was your calculator making things up.

Why cut at all

Say you measure a cube with a ruler and get 3.42 cm by 2.1 cm. That second measurement means "somewhere between 2.05 and 2.15." Multiply the extremes and the area lands anywhere from 7.011 to 7.353 cm². You don't even know the tenths place for certain, so reporting 7.182 cm² — three decimal places, five digits — is a claim you cannot back up.

Significant figure rules are a fast, mechanical way to answer one question: how much of my calculator's answer did I actually earn? Uncertainty doesn't vanish in a calculation. It travels through, and it always wins.

The one habit that matters: carry all your digits through the middle of a multi-step calculation, and round only once, at the very end. Rounding at every step stacks small errors on top of each other.

Count decimal places

The answer gets the same number of decimal places as the input with the fewest. Sums, differences, and averages of raw data follow this one.

It's two steps. First find the weakest input, count its significant digits, and cut the calculator's output there. Then round the last surviving digit up or down depending on the first digit you threw away — five or more rounds up.

A different rule

A quick word on adding and subtracting

Nearly everything you calculate in this course — area, volume, density, percent error — is multiplication and division, so the rule above is the one you'll live in. Addition and subtraction follow a different rule. You should know it exists and be able to spot when it applies.

Line the measurements up by their decimal points. Whichever one runs out of digits first sets the wall, and the answer stops there. It counts decimal places, not significant digits:

The hatched squares are places nobody measured. 12.7 g stopped at the tenths place, so the sum does too: 18.1 g. Notice it would be wrong to keep three decimals just because one measurement happened to have them.

One consequence worth remembering. 100.0 g − 97.65 g = 2.35 g, which rounds to 2.4 g — two four-digit measurements produced a two-digit answer. Nothing went wrong. You subtracted away almost everything you knew and kept only a small remainder, where the uncertainty is now a large fraction of what's left. This is exactly what happens inside a percent error calculation, so it comes back later on this page.
Counts · constants · conversions

Numbers that don't limit you

Not every number in a calculation is a measurement. Some are perfect, and perfect numbers never limit your significant digits.

  • Counted objects. Ten pennies in a graduated cylinder is not "about ten" — it is exactly ten. Divide the volume of 10 pennies by 10 and that 10 is exact, so your answer keeps every significant digit the volume had.
  • Defined conversions. 1 cm³ = 1 mL exactly, and 100 cm = 1 m exactly. Converting units never costs you digits. This is why a density in g/mL and one in g/cm³ are the same number.
  • Constants like π. Use more digits of π than you need — your calculator's button is fine — and let the measurements decide the answer. In V = πr²ℓ, only r and ℓ control the significant digits.
  • Exact numbers in a formula. The 2 in r = d/2 and the 100 in a percent are definitions, not measurements. A diameter of 1.27 cm gives a radius of 0.635 cm — still three significant digits.
Why massing in batches is smarter. Massing 10 identical objects and dividing by 10 is not a shortcut for lazy measuring — it's a real precision upgrade. Your balance is uncertain by about ±0.01 g no matter what's on the pan. Spread that uncertainty across 10 objects and each one is only uncertain by about ±0.001 g. That's the whole reason to weigh in batches.

Repeated measurements

Averaging identical objects

An average is a sum followed by a division. The division is by a count, and a count is exact, so it costs you nothing. Mass ten identical objects at once and divide by ten, and the average keeps every significant digit the total had.

There is a second, better reason to do it that way. A balance is uncertain by roughly the same amount whatever sits on the pan — say ±0.01 g. Weigh one object and your answer carries all of that uncertainty. Weigh ten and divide, and the uncertainty gets divided too, down to about ±0.001 g per object. Batch weighing buys precision you cannot get any other way.

Comparing to a target

Percent error

Percent error asks how far off you landed, as a fraction of the value you were aiming at:

% error = |measured − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100

Take the absolute value — percent error is a size, not a direction, so it is never negative. The 100 is a definition, so it is exact and never limits your digits. In every problem below, treat the accepted value as an exact target.

Watch what happens to your digits along the way. You subtract first, and that difference follows the decimal-place rule from earlier — which often leaves you with very few significant digits. Then you divide, and that small count is what limits the final answer.
