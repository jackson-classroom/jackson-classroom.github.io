Unit 4 · Molar mass and molar volume

Compounds by the gram, gases by the liter.

On the last page, you read an element’s molar mass straight off the periodic table. Compounds aren’t on the table, so you build their molar mass by adding up the atoms in the formula. Gases come with a shortcut of their own: at STP, one mole of any gas takes up 22.4 liters.

Step one

Counting atoms in a formula

A chemical formula is a recipe for one molecule, or one formula unit, of a compound. The symbols tell you which elements are in it, and the subscripts tell you how many atoms of each. Before you can find a molar mass, you have to count the atoms correctly.

H2O

A subscript counts the atom right before it

The 2 belongs to H only. No subscript means 1.

2 H + 1 O = 3 atoms
Ca(NO3)2

A subscript after parentheses multiplies everything inside

Two whole NO3 groups, so 2 N and 6 O.

1 Ca + 2 N + 6 O = 9 atoms
2 H2O

A coefficient counts whole formulas

The big 2 in front means two water molecules, or two moles of water. It isn’t part of the formula, so leave it out of the molar mass.

The method

Molar mass of a compound

One mole of water is 6.02 × 1023 water molecules. Every one of them has 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom, so a mole of water contains 2 mol of H atoms and 1 mol of O atoms. Its mass is the mass of all those atoms added together. Build a mole of each compound on the scale.

Build one mole on the scale

Each ball on the scale is one mole of atoms of that element: 6.02 × 1023 atoms.

That’s the whole method. The molar mass of a compound is the sum of the molar masses of all the atoms in its formula. Its unit is g/mol, the same as for an element.

Four steps

  1. Count the atoms of each element in the formula.
  2. Look up each element’s molar mass on the periodic table.
  3. Multiply each molar mass by its number of atoms.
  4. Add. Keep two decimal places, the same as the periodic table.
Worked example

Molecules and formula units

Water and sugar are made of separate molecules. Ionic compounds like table salt, NaCl, aren’t. They’re a huge lattice of Na+ and Cl− ions, so the smallest repeating unit is called a formula unit: one Na+ and one Cl−. Molar mass works exactly the same way for both.

SubstanceOne particleOne mole (6.02 × 1023 particles)
Water, H2O1 molecule: 18.02 amu18.02 g
Carbon dioxide, CO21 molecule: 44.01 amu44.01 g
Sodium chloride, NaCl1 formula unit: 58.44 amu58.44 g
Oxygen gas, O21 molecule: 32.00 amu32.00 g

Same number, different unit, just like the elements on the last page: amu for one particle, grams for one mole.

Diatomic elements are molecules too. Seven elements exist as two-atom molecules: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2 and I2. “Oxygen gas” means O2, so its molar mass is 2 × 16.00 = 32.00 g/mol, not 16.00.
Gases

Molar volume at STP

Gases are hard to weigh but easy to measure by volume. And gases have a surprising property: at the same temperature and pressure, one mole of any gas takes up the same volume. Hydrogen, helium, carbon dioxide: it doesn’t matter which gas it is.

To compare gases fairly, chemists use one standard set of conditions called STP, short for standard temperature and pressure.

0 °C

Standard temperature

The freezing point of water, which is 273 K.

1 atm

Standard pressure

Normal air pressure at sea level: 101.3 kPa, or 760 mmHg.

22.4 L/mol

Molar volume at STP

One mole of any gas at STP takes up 22.4 L. That number is the gas’s molar volume.

Why one number works for every gas

In a solid or a liquid, the particles touch, so a mole of big particles takes up more room than a mole of small ones. A gas is different. Its particles are far apart, and at STP the particles themselves fill less than 0.1% of the space. Whether each particle is a tiny helium atom or a bigger CO2 molecule hardly matters. What sets the volume is how many particles there are.

How far apart, to scale

The average distance between neighboring particles, drawn to scale. In every direction, gas particles at STP are about 11 times farther apart than the molecules in liquid water. That makes the volume about 11 × 11 × 11 times bigger, more than a thousand times.

Same moles, same volume

A gas at STP
Moles of gas

Each drawn particle stands for an enormous number of real ones, and they’re drawn far bigger than scale. Lighter particles move faster, but at the same temperature and pressure a mole of any gas still takes up the same volume.

Only gases, and only at STP. 22.4 L/mol doesn’t work for solids or liquids: a mole of liquid water fills just 18.0 mL. And a gas at a different temperature or pressure has a different molar volume. Every gas problem on this page is at STP.

Some newer textbooks define STP with a pressure of 1 bar instead of 1 atm, which gives 22.7 L/mol. This page uses 0 °C, 1 atm and 22.4 L/mol.

Compare

Same volume, different mass

A mole of any gas at STP takes up 22.4 L, but the mass of that mole depends on the gas. It’s the idea from the last page again: same count, different mass. Below are 22.4 L of nine gases, one mole each, compared with 22.4 L of air. Air is a mixture, mostly N2 and O2, and 22.4 L of it at STP has a mass of about 29.0 g. A balloon of any gas lighter than that floats.

22.4 L of each gas at STP

Each balloon: 22.4 L = 1.00 mol. The same 22.4 L of air: about 29.0 g.

The masses are the gas only, not the balloon. Nitrogen is most of the air, so a nitrogen balloon is about as heavy as the air it pushes aside.

Real-world chemistry. Natural gas is mostly methane, CH4 (16.05 g/mol), which is lighter than air and rises. Propane, C3H8 (44.11 g/mol), is heavier than air and sinks. That’s why natural-gas detectors are mounted high on a wall, and propane detectors go down low.

The factor-label method

  1. Write what you’re given, with its unit and formula: 88.0 g CO2, not just 88.0.
  2. Find the molar mass first if the problem has grams in it.
  3. Multiply by a conversion factor with the unit you have on the bottom.
  4. Cancel units. The unit left over should be the one you want.
  5. Do the math, round to the right number of significant figures, and sense-check the size of the answer.

Common mistakes

Watch-outs

Skipping the subscripts

Every subscript multiplies that element’s molar mass.

H2O: 1.01 + 16.00 = 17.01 g/mol
H2O: 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol

Stopping at the parentheses

A subscript after parentheses multiplies every atom inside them.

Ca(NO3)2: 40.08 + 14.01 + 3(16.00) = 102.09
Ca(NO3)2: 40.08 + 2(14.01) + 6(16.00) = 164.10

Forgetting a gas is diatomic

Oxygen gas is O2 molecules, not single O atoms. The same goes for H2, N2, F2, Cl2, Br2 and I2.

Oxygen gas: 16.00 g/mol
Oxygen gas, O2: 32.00 g/mol

Using 22.4 L for a liquid or solid

22.4 L/mol is only for gases, and only at STP. Particles in liquids and solids touch.

1 mol H2O(l) = 22.4 L
1 mol H2O(l) = 18.0 mL

Multiplying liters by 22.4

Going from liters to moles, you divide. Check the units: L × L/mol leaves L²/mol, which isn’t moles.

11.2 L × 22.4 L/mol = 251 L²/mol
11.2 L ÷ 22.4 L/mol = 0.500 mol

Mixing up liters and grams

22.4 is liters per mole, and it’s the same for every gas. Molar mass is grams per mole, and it depends on the formula.

44.8 L CO2 = 44.8 g CO2
44.8 L CO2 = 2.00 mol = 88.0 g CO2

Putting the coefficient in the molar mass

A coefficient tells you how many moles, not what one mole weighs.

2 H2O: molar mass 36.04 g/mol
H2O: 18.02 g/mol, so 2 mol H2O = 36.04 g
