Two-thirds of the atoms, one-ninth of the mass.

Two of the three atoms in a water molecule are hydrogen. But hydrogen atoms are so light that they make up only about 11% of water’s mass. Percent composition tells you how a compound’s mass is split among its elements. You already have the tools to find it: molar mass from the last page, and the percents you use every day.

Percent by mass
Each percent is one element’s share of the mass. Together they always add up to 100%.

Water, H2O. A hydrogen atom has about 1/16 the mass of an oxygen atom, so the two hydrogens make up only 11.21% of the mass.

The idea

Percent by mass

You already know how a percent works. Score 45 points out of 50 on a quiz, and your grade is 45 ÷ 50 × 100 = 90%. It’s the part divided by the whole, times 100.

The percent composition of a compound is the same idea with mass. It gives the percent by mass of each element in the compound: the mass of that element divided by the mass of the whole compound, times 100.

Big sample or small, the percents don’t change

Every molecule of water is the same H2O, so every sample of water has the same makeup. That’s true of every compound, and chemists call it the law of definite proportions. Change the size of the sample and watch what changes and what doesn’t.

By mass, not by atoms

Percent composition is always by mass. Look back at the water figure at the top of the page. Two-thirds of the atoms in water are hydrogen, but an oxygen atom has about 16 times the mass of a hydrogen atom. So by mass, water is mostly oxygen: 88.79%. Counting atoms gives you a different, wrong answer.

The method

Percent composition from a formula

If you know a compound’s formula, you don’t need a sample at all. Picture one mole of it. Its mass is the molar mass, and you already know how much of that mass comes from each element: it’s each row of the molar mass table from the last page.

Four steps

  1. Find the mass of each element in one mole: count its atoms and multiply by its molar mass.
  2. Add them up to get the molar mass of the compound. That’s the whole.
  3. Divide each element’s mass by the molar mass, then multiply by 100. Keep two decimal places.
  4. Check that the percents add up to 100%.
Worked example

Rounding. Rounded percents sometimes add up to 99.99% or 100.01%. That’s just rounding. A total like 80% or 115% means something went wrong, so go back and check.

From data

Percent composition from lab data

Sometimes you start with a sample and a balance instead of a formula. You find the mass of each element in the sample, and the math is the same as before: part ÷ whole × 100. The whole is always the mass of the compound.

Lab: burning magnesium

Magnesium ribbon burns in air to form magnesium oxide: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. The magnesium picks up oxygen from the air, so the white powder left at the end weighs more than the ribbon did. The extra mass is the oxygen. Run the lab, then find the percent of magnesium in the product.

Lab: burn a magnesium ribbon

% Mg

In a real lab, some of the magnesium reacts with nitrogen in the air instead, and a little white smoke escapes. Both make the product lighter, so real results for percent magnesium usually come out a little high.

Three kinds of data problems

Data problems give you the masses in different ways. Whatever you’re given, find the mass of each element and the mass of the whole compound first. Then divide.

Using it

Percent as a conversion factor

Iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, is 69.94% iron by mass. That means every 100 g of Fe2O3 contains 69.94 g of iron. Written as a fraction, that’s a conversion factor, and it works either way up:

69.94% iron in Fe2O3

Use it like any other conversion factor: start with what you’re given, and put the unit you have on the bottom so it cancels.

Worked example

Another way to write the same factor. One mole of Fe2O3 has 111.70 g of iron in 159.70 g of compound. The factor (111.70 g Fe / 159.70 g Fe2O3) is the same ratio as (69.94 g Fe / 100 g Fe2O3), so either one gives the same answer.
Compare

Which one has the most?

Plants need nitrogen to grow, and farmers can buy it in several different compounds. The three numbers on a bag of fertilizer are percents by mass, and the first one is nitrogen. So a bag of urea is labeled 46-0-0. The higher the percent, the less fertilizer a farmer has to buy, haul and spread to get the same amount of nitrogen. Iron ores work the same way.

Common mistakes

Watch-outs

Counting atoms instead of mass

Percent composition is by mass. Atoms of different elements have different masses.

H2O: 2 of 3 atoms, so 66.7% H
H2O: 2.02 ÷ 18.02 × 100 = 11.21% H

Skipping the subscript

The mass of an element in one mole is its number of atoms times its molar mass.

H in H2O: 1.01 ÷ 18.02 × 100 = 5.60%
H in H2O: 2(1.01) ÷ 18.02 × 100 = 11.21%

Dividing by the other element

A compound with 8.20 g Mg and 5.40 g O. Divide by the whole compound, not by the other element.

8.20 ÷ 5.40 × 100 = 152% Mg
8.20 ÷ 13.60 × 100 = 60.3% Mg

Adding the product to what made it

Reacting 1.20 g of Al with oxygen forms 2.27 g of aluminum oxide. The aluminum is already part of the 2.27 g.

1.20 ÷ (1.20 + 2.27) × 100 = 34.6% Al
1.20 ÷ 2.27 × 100 = 52.9% Al

Dividing upside down

A part can’t be bigger than the whole. Any percent over 100% is a red flag.

18.02 ÷ 2.02 × 100 = 892% H
2.02 ÷ 18.02 × 100 = 11.21% H

Using the percent as a plain number

69.94% means 69.94 g per 100 g. Divide by 100, or write it as a factor.

75.0 g Fe2O3 × 69.94 = 5.25 × 103 g Fe
75.0 g × 69.94 ÷ 100 = 52.5 g Fe

Not checking the total

The percents must add up to 100%. If they don’t, one of them is wrong.

Fe2O3: 69.94% Fe + 10.02% O = 79.96%
Fe2O3: 69.94% Fe + 30.06% O = 100.00%
