One ratio, many molecules.

On the last page, you found a compound’s empirical formula, the simplest ratio of its atoms. But a ratio isn’t the whole story. Formaldehyde, the acid in vinegar and glucose all have the empirical formula CH2O. To find the molecular formula, the real number of atoms in one molecule, you need one more measurement: the molar mass. Then you’ll use the same mole ratios on hydrates, crystals with water locked inside.

Glucose is six CH2O units. Dividing its molar mass by the mass of one unit tells you how many there are.

The idea

Empirical formula vs. molecular formula

An empirical formula gives the simplest ratio of atoms. For an ionic compound like NaCl or Fe2O3, that’s all there is to say. An ionic crystal is one big repeating pattern of ions, and its formula gives their ratio.

A molecular compound is different. It’s made of separate molecules, and every molecule has a definite number of atoms. The molecular formula gives that actual number. A molecule of hydrogen peroxide has 2 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms, so its molecular formula is H2O2. The ratio 2 : 2 reduces to 1 : 1, so its empirical formula is HO. But there’s no HO molecule in the bottle.

HO

Empirical formula

H2O2

Molecular formula

The actual number of each atom in one molecule. It’s what the compound really is.

× n

The link between them

The molecular formula is the empirical formula with every subscript multiplied by the same whole number, n. Here, n = 2.

Same percents, different compounds

Percent composition depends only on the ratio of the atoms. So every compound with the empirical formula CH2O has exactly the same percent composition. Percents alone can’t tell you whether you have formaldehyde or glucose. Step through the family and watch what changes and what doesn’t.

Sometimes n = 1

Many molecular formulas can’t be reduced. Water is H2O, carbon dioxide is CO2 and propane is C3H8. For these, the empirical formula and the molecular formula are the same, and n = 1.

The method

Finding the molecular formula

To get from the empirical formula to the molecular formula, you need the compound’s molar mass. It comes from a separate experiment, and a problem will give it to you. One molecule is a whole number of empirical units, so the molar mass is a whole number of empirical formula masses.

n = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass
  1. Find the empirical formula, if you don’t already have it. Use the method from the last page: percent to mass, mass to mole, divide by small, multiply ’til whole.
  2. Find the empirical formula mass. Add up the atomic masses in the empirical formula, the same way you find any molar mass.
  3. Divide. The compound’s molar mass ÷ the empirical formula mass = n. It should come out very close to a whole number.
  4. Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by n.
Worked example

n is a whole number. With real data, n might come out 5.98 or 2.01. Round it. If it comes out 2.5 or 1.33, don’t round. Something went wrong earlier, so recheck the empirical formula and its mass.

A new kind of formula

Hydrates

Some ionic compounds trap water molecules inside their crystals as they form. The water isn’t just dampness on the surface. It’s part of the crystal structure, and it’s there in a fixed ratio. A compound like this is called a hydrate, and the water in it is called the water of hydration.

The bright blue crystals of copper(II) sulfate are a hydrate. For every formula unit of CuSO4, the crystal holds exactly 5 water molecules:

The molar mass of a hydrate

The water is part of the compound, so it’s part of the molar mass. Find the molar mass of the salt, and then add 18.02 g/mol for every water.

CuSO4·5H2O: 159.61 + 5(18.02) = 159.61 + 90.10 = 249.71 g/mol

From there, the percent water is a percent composition problem, with the water as the part and the hydrate as the whole:

90.10 ÷ 249.71 × 100 = 36.08% water

Heating a hydrate

The water of hydration is held loosely. Heat a hydrate and the water leaves as steam. The anhydrous salt stays behind:

CuSO4·5H2O(s) → CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(g)

The anhydrous salt often looks different. Blue CuSO4·5H2O turns into a white powder. Add a few drops of water and the blue comes back. Cobalt(II) chloride changes from blue to pink as it picks up water, so paper soaked in it is used to show humidity. Anhydrous calcium chloride pulls water out of the air so strongly that it’s sold to keep closets and basements dry.

From data

Finding the formula of a hydrate

How do you know it’s 5 waters and not 4 or 6? Weigh a sample of the hydrate, heat it until the water is gone, and weigh what’s left. The mass that was lost is water. From there, it’s the same idea as an empirical formula. The difference is that you compare the moles of two compounds, the salt and the water, instead of two elements.

  1. Find both masses. Mass of water = mass of the hydrate − mass of the anhydrous salt.
  2. Mass to mole. Divide the mass of the salt by the salt’s molar mass. Divide the mass of the water by 18.02 g/mol.
  3. Divide by the moles of salt. Moles of water ÷ moles of salt = x, the number of waters for every 1 formula unit of salt.
  4. Round x and write the formula: the salt, a dot, then x H2O.

Real results are rarely this clean. If you stop heating too soon, some water is still in the crystals and x comes out too low. That’s why chemists heat, cool, weigh, and then heat again until the mass stops changing. If you heat too strongly, the salt itself can start to break down, and x comes out too high.

Three kinds of hydrate problems

A problem might give you the mass before and after heating, the mass of water lost, or the percent water. Whatever you’re given, find the mass of the salt and the mass of the water first. Then it’s the same method.

Worked example

The salt gets the 1. A hydrate’s formula is written for 1 formula unit of salt, so always divide by the moles of salt. That’s also the smaller number whenever x is more than 1.
Common mistakes

Watch-outs

Stopping at the empirical formula

If the problem gives a molar mass, it wants the molecular formula. Hydrogen peroxide’s molar mass is 34.02 g/mol.

HO (17.01 g/mol)
34.02 ÷ 17.01 = 2, so H2O2

Dividing upside down

The molar mass goes on top. n is a whole number, never a fraction less than 1.

13.02 ÷ 78.12 = 0.167
78.12 ÷ 13.02 = 6

Multiplying just one subscript

Multiply every subscript by n, including the ones that are 1.

CH2O × 6 → C6H2O
CH2O × 6 → C6H12O6

Adding n instead of multiplying

n counts whole copies of the empirical formula, so it multiplies.

NO2, n = 2 → N3O4
NO2, n = 2 → N2O4

Leaving the water out of the molar mass

The water of hydration is part of the compound. The dot means “combined with,” so add.

CuSO4·5H2O = 159.61 g/mol
159.61 + 5(18.02) = 249.71 g/mol

Using what’s left as the water

5.00 g of a hydrate is heated and 2.44 g is left. What’s left is the salt. The water is what was lost.

2.44 g of water
5.00 − 2.44 = 2.56 g of water

Using the hydrate’s mass for the salt

The 5.00 g includes the water. Only the 2.44 g left after heating is MgSO4.

5.00 ÷ 120.37 = 0.04154 mol MgSO4
2.44 ÷ 120.37 = 0.02027 mol MgSO4

Dividing the ratio upside down

x is waters per salt, so the moles of water go on top.

0.02027 ÷ 0.1421 = 0.143
0.1421 ÷ 0.02027 = 7.01, so x = 7
