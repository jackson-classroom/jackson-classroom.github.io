Unit 4 · The mole

Counting atoms by weighing them.

Atoms are far too small to count one at a time. So chemists count them the way a hardware store counts nails: they weigh a pile. The pile chemists use is called a mole, and it always holds the same number of atoms: 6.02 × 1023.

The idea

A mole is a number

A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen. A dozen eggs is 12 eggs, and a dozen atoms is 12 atoms. In the same way, a mole of eggs is 6.02 × 1023 eggs, and a mole of atoms is 6.02 × 1023 atoms. That number is called Avogadro’s number, after the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro.

The unit

The symbol for mole is mol. Two and a half moles of iron is written 2.5 mol Fe.

Why chemists count by weighing

One carbon atom has a mass of about 0.0000000000000000000000199 g. No one could count atoms that small one at a time. But if you know how much one of something weighs, you can count a whole pile just by weighing it. Try it with pennies first. Every U.S. penny made since 1983 has a mass of 2.50 g.

That’s the whole idea of the mole. Chemists don’t want to divide by 1.994 × 10−23 g every time, so they bundle atoms into moles. 12.01 g of carbon is always one mole of carbon atoms, so 36.03 g is three moles. Each element has its own “scoop” mass, which you’ll find on the periodic table further down this page.

Reading 6.02 × 1023

Numbers this big and this small are written in scientific notation. The exponent tells you how many places to move the decimal point: to the right when it’s positive, to the left when it’s negative. Slide the exponent and watch the decimal point move.

Move the decimal point

Avogadro’s number, exactly. Since 2019, one mole has been defined as exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 particles. This page rounds it to 6.02 × 1023, which is plenty for three significant figures. Chemists celebrate Mole Day every October 23 (10/23), from 6:02 a.m. to 6:02 p.m.
Scale

How big is a mole?

Written out, 6.02 × 1023 is 602 sextillion. It’s hard to picture a number that big. A billion feels huge, but it’s only 109. To get from a billion to a mole, you have to multiply by 10 almost fifteen more times. Climb the ladder one step at a time.

    The key idea

    Same count, different mass

    A dozen eggs and a dozen bowling balls have the same count, but you’d never mix up their masses. Moles work the same way. A mole of any element has 6.02 × 1023 atoms, but the mass of that mole depends on how heavy each atom is.

    One mole of each, drawn to scale

    Every sample holds 6.02 × 1023 atoms. They’re all about the size of a sugar cube, but the lead cube has 17 times the mass of the carbon cube. And a mole of mercury, a liquid metal, fills about one tablespoon (14.8 mL).

    Put them on a balance

    Load a pan on each side. First compare one mole of each. Then switch to equal masses and see what happens to the number of atoms.

    Two-pan balance

    Equal moles means equal atoms. Equal masses does not. Lighter atoms means more of them fit into the same mass: 50.0 g of carbon holds about 16 times as many atoms as 50.0 g of gold.
    The bridge

    Molar mass

    The molar mass of an element is the mass of one mole of its atoms. Its unit is grams per mole (g/mol). You don’t have to memorize any molar masses. Each one is printed on the periodic table as the element’s average atomic mass.

    26FeIron55.85
    26 is the atomic number. It’s the number of protons in an iron atom. It is not the molar mass.
    55.85 is the average atomic mass. One iron atom: 55.85 amu. One mole of iron atoms: 55.85 g. So the molar mass of iron is 55.85 g/mol.

    Some periodic tables print the atomic mass above the symbol and some below. The molar mass is always the number with decimals. The decimals are there because it’s an average over the element’s isotopes.

    Why the same number works twice

    Atomic masses are measured in atomic mass units (amu), and 1 amu is tiny: 1.66 × 10−24 g. It takes 6.02 × 1023 amu to make one gram. That’s no coincidence. Avogadro’s number was picked so that it would work out this way. So whatever one atom weighs in amu, a mole of those atoms weighs the same number of grams.

    ElementOne atomOne mole (6.02 × 1023 atoms)
    Carbon, C12.01 amu12.01 g
    Aluminum, Al26.98 amu26.98 g
    Iron, Fe55.85 amu55.85 g
    Gold, Au196.97 amu196.97 g

    Same number, different unit: amu for one atom, grams for one mole. That’s what you saw in the zoom-out above. The balance read 12.01 g at exactly one mole of carbon.

    Molar mass is a conversion factor

    “The molar mass of iron is 55.85 g/mol” means 55.85 g Fe = 1 mol Fe. You can write that as a fraction either way up, and both are equal to 1. Avogadro’s number works the same way: 6.02 × 1023 atoms = 1 mol.

    The method

    Converting: the mole map

    Grams, moles and atoms are three ways to describe the same sample. Moles sit in the middle. Molar mass connects grams to moles, and Avogadro’s number connects moles to atoms. There is no direct road from grams to atoms. You always go through moles.

    The factor-label method

    1. Write what you’re given, with its unit and element: 25.0 g Fe, not just 25.0.
    2. Multiply by a conversion factor with the unit you have on the bottom.
    3. Cancel units that appear on the top and bottom. The unit left over should be the one you want.
    4. Do the math: multiply across the top, divide by the bottom, and round to the right number of significant figures.
    5. Sense check. More than one mole means more than 6.02 × 1023 atoms, and more than one molar mass of grams.
    Worked example

    Typing 6.02 × 1023 on a calculator. Use the scientific-notation key: EE on a TI-84 (press 2nd, then the comma key), ×10ˣ on a TI-30, or EXP on many others. It shows up as 6.02E23. If you type × 10 ^ 23 instead, put the whole number in parentheses when you divide by it: 3.01 × 1023 ÷ (6.02 × 1023).
    Common mistakes

    Watch-outs

    “A mole of gold has more atoms”

    A mole is a count, so every mole has the same number of atoms. A mole of gold just has more mass, because each gold atom is heavier.

    1 mol Au has more atoms than 1 mol C
    1 mol Au = 1 mol C = 6.02 × 1023 atoms

    “Same mass, same atoms”

    Equal masses only have equal atoms if the atoms weigh the same. Lighter atoms means more of them.

    10 g C and 10 g Au: same number of atoms
    10 g C has about 16 times as many atoms

    Using the atomic number

    The whole number at the top of the tile counts protons. Molar mass is the number with decimals.

    Fe: 26 g/mol
    Fe: 55.85 g/mol

    Multiplying grams by molar mass

    Going from grams to moles, you divide. Check the units: g × g/mol leaves g²/mol, which isn’t moles.

    10.0 g × 40.08 g/mol = 401 g²/mol
    10.0 g ÷ 40.08 g/mol = 0.250 mol

    The calculator trap

    Typing ÷ 6.02 × 10 ^ 23 without parentheses divides by 6.02, then multiplies by 1023.

    3.01 × 1023 ÷ 6.02 × 10^23 = 5 × 1045
    3.01 × 1023 ÷ (6.02 × 1023) = 0.500 mol

    One atom isn’t 12.01 g

    12.01 is a carbon atom’s mass in amu. In grams, one carbon atom is 1.99 × 10−23 g. It takes a whole mole of carbon atoms to make 12.01 g.
