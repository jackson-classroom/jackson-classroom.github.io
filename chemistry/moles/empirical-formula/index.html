The idea

The simplest whole-number ratio

A formula is a recipe written in atoms. Fe2O3 means 2 iron atoms for every 3 oxygen atoms. Scale that up to moles and the ratio doesn’t change: 2 mol of iron for every 3 mol of oxygen.

When chemists make a new compound, nobody hands them its formula. They can’t count its atoms, but they can weigh things, and molar mass turns grams into moles. The ratio of the moles is the ratio of the atoms. A formula found this way is called the empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a compound. Empirical means “based on measurement.”

mol : mol

Moles count atoms

A mole is a number of particles. 2 mol of H for every 1 mol of O means 2 H atoms for every 1 O atom.

whole

Whole numbers only

A formula counts whole atoms. There’s no such thing as half an atom, so every subscript is a whole number.

simplest

As small as possible

Reduce the ratio as far as it goes, the way you’d reduce a fraction. 2 : 4 is written 1 : 2.

Moles, not grams

Sulfur dioxide is 50.05% sulfur and 49.95% oxygen. That’s half and half by mass, but the formula isn’t SO. A sulfur atom has about twice the mass of an oxygen atom, so the same mass of oxygen holds about twice as many atoms. Dividing by molar mass shows it: 1.561 mol of S and 3.122 mol of O, a 1 : 2 ratio. The formula is SO2.

This is the last page’s big idea in reverse. There, counting atoms gave the wrong percents. Here, comparing grams gives the wrong formula. You have to go through moles.

The method

Empirical formula from percent composition

The method works for any compound, with two elements or five. Chemistry students have memorized it as a rhyme for years:

Percent to mass, mass to mole, divide by small, multiply ’til whole.
  1. Percent to mass. Assume you have exactly 100 g of the compound. Then each percent is a mass in grams: 69.94% Fe becomes 69.94 g Fe.
  2. Mass to mole. Divide each mass by the element’s molar mass. Keep four significant figures. Don’t round to whole numbers yet.
  3. Divide by small. Divide every number of moles by the smallest one. The smallest becomes exactly 1.
  4. Multiply ’til whole. If every number is within about 0.1 of a whole number, round. If not, multiply every number by 2, 3, 4 or 5 until they’re all whole. Those whole numbers are the subscripts.
Worked example

Why 100 g? Every sample of a compound has the same percent composition, so any sample size would give the same formula. With 100 g, each percent is already a number of grams, so there’s nothing to calculate.

The tricky step

Multiply ’til whole

After you divide by the smallest, the numbers should be close to whole numbers. Measurements are never perfect, so a value like 1.98 or 3.04 is normal. Round it. But a value like 1.50 or 2.33 isn’t an error. It’s a fraction in disguise.

Look at Fe2O3: 2 Fe for every 3 O. Divide both by the smallest, 2, and you get 1 : 1.5. To get back to whole numbers, multiply every number by 2. The decimal tells you what to multiply by:

≈ 1.98

Just round

Within about 0.1 of a whole number is normal measurement error. 1.98 rounds to 2, and 3.04 rounds to 3.

.50

Multiply by 2

Halves. 1.50 × 2 = 3, and 2.50 × 2 = 5.

.33 or .67

Multiply by 3

Thirds. 1.33 × 3 = 4, and 2.67 × 3 = 8.

.25 or .75

Multiply by 4

Quarters. 1.25 × 4 = 5, and 2.25 × 4 = 9.

.2 · .4 · .6 · .8

Multiply by 5

Fifths. 1.20 × 5 = 6, and 2.40 × 5 = 12.

Multiply every number by the same factor, including the 1. Multiplying just one of them changes the ratio.

Never round 1.5 to 2. Round Fe 1 : O 1.5 to 1 : 2 and you get FeO2, which would be 63.57% Fe, not the 69.94% you started with. Rounding a fraction gives you the formula of a different compound, or of one that doesn’t exist.

From data

Empirical formula from lab data

A lab doesn’t give you percents. It gives you masses. That saves a step: skip “percent to mass” and go straight to moles. You just need the mass of each element, which sometimes takes a subtraction first.

Lab: copper and sulfur

When copper is heated with sulfur, the two combine into a copper sulfide. Copper can form more than one compound with sulfur, so which one is this? You can’t tell by looking. Run the lab, then let the masses decide.

Lab: heat copper with sulfur

Real results are rarely this clean. If some of the copper doesn’t react, the product holds too little sulfur and the ratio comes out above 2 : 1. That’s why the lab uses more sulfur than the copper needs.

Common mistakes

Watch-outs

Comparing grams instead of moles

Equal masses don’t mean equal numbers of atoms. Sulfur dioxide is about half sulfur and half oxygen by mass.

50.05 g S : 49.95 g O ≈ 1 : 1, so SO
1.561 mol S : 3.122 mol O = 1 : 2, so SO2

Using percents as subscripts

Subscripts count atoms, not grams. Turn the percents into moles first.

69.94% Fe, 30.06% O: Fe70O30
1.252 mol Fe : 1.879 mol O, so Fe2O3

Using 32.00 g/mol for oxygen

You’re counting O atoms in the compound, not O2 molecules. The same goes for N, H and the halogens.

30.06 g ÷ 32.00 = 0.9394 mol O
30.06 g ÷ 16.00 = 1.879 mol O

Dividing upside down

Moles = grams ÷ molar mass. The grams go on top.

55.85 ÷ 69.94 = 0.7985 mol Fe
69.94 ÷ 55.85 = 1.252 mol Fe

Rounding a fraction

Only round a number within about 0.1 of a whole number. 1.50 is exactly halfway.

Fe 1 : O 1.50 → Fe 1 : O 2, so FeO2
× 2 → Fe 2 : O 3, so Fe2O3

Multiplying just one number

Multiply every number by the same factor, or the ratio changes.

Fe 1 : O 1.50 × 2 → FeO3
Fe 1 × 2 : O 1.50 × 2 → Fe2O3

Using the compound’s mass for an element

1.20 g of aluminum reacts with oxygen to make 2.27 g of product. The 2.27 g includes the aluminum.

1.20 g Al and 2.27 g O
1.20 g Al and 2.27 − 1.20 = 1.07 g O
