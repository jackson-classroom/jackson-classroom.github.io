Unit 4 · Mole ratios

Coefficients count moles.

So far, you’ve used the mole to count the particles in one substance. A balanced equation connects one substance to another. Its coefficients tell you how many moles of each substance react or form, so you can figure out how much ammonia you’ll get from 6 moles of hydrogen before you ever go into the lab.

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3
The coefficients are a ratio. It holds for molecules, dozens or moles, at any size.

One N2 molecule and three H2 molecules regroup into two NH3 molecules. Same atoms, new partners.

The idea

Reading an equation in moles

A balanced equation is a recipe. Take the reaction that makes ammonia, N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3. The coefficients say that 1 molecule of nitrogen reacts with 3 molecules of hydrogen to make 2 molecules of ammonia. A coefficient with no number written is a 1.

Nobody runs a reaction one molecule at a time, though. Scale the recipe up. Make it twice and you use 2 N2 and 6 H2 to make 4 NH3. Make it a dozen times and it’s 1 dozen N2, 3 dozen H2 and 2 dozen NH3. A mole is a counting number, just like a dozen, so the same is true in moles: 1 mol N2 reacts with 3 mol H2 to make 2 mol NH3.

Coefficients count particles, so they count moles.
Scale up the recipe

Atoms add up. Moles don’t have to.

Count the atoms on each side of any balanced equation and they match. That’s what balancing means, and it’s why mass is conserved. Now count the molecules. In the ammonia reaction, 4 molecules go in and only 2 come out. The atoms regrouped into fewer, bigger molecules. So the moles of reactants don’t have to equal the moles of products. Only the atoms have to match.

The tool

Mole ratios

A mole ratio compares the moles of two substances in a balanced equation. Take the coefficients of the two substances and write them as a fraction. In N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3, every 3 mol of H2 makes 2 mol of NH3, so you can write the ratio either way up:

A mole ratio is a conversion factor, like the ones you used with molar mass. There are two differences. It links two different substances, so it’s the bridge from one to the other. And it’s only true for this one reaction. Every pair of substances in an equation has a mole ratio, even two reactants or two products.

The method

Moles of one substance to moles of another

This is one more factor-label problem. You start with the moles you’re given, multiply by one conversion factor, and the units cancel to leave the moles you want.

Wanted on top, given on the bottom.
  1. Check that the equation is balanced. The mole ratio comes from the coefficients, so they have to be right.
  2. Start with what you’re given: the number of moles and the substance.
  3. Multiply by the mole ratio. The coefficient and formula of the substance you want go on top. The coefficient and formula of the substance you’re given go on the bottom, so it cancels.
  4. Do the math. Multiply by the top coefficient, divide by the bottom one, and round to the same number of significant figures as the amount you were given.
Worked example

Coefficients are exact. A 3 in an equation means exactly 3, not “about 3.” Like the 12 in a dozen, coefficients never limit significant figures. Your answer gets the same number of significant figures as the amount you were given: 0.250 mol × 3 ÷ 2 = 0.375 mol, not 0.4 mol.
Sense check. Compare the two coefficients. If the substance you want has the bigger coefficient, your answer should be bigger than the number you started with. If it has the smaller coefficient, your answer should be smaller.
Before you start

Balance first

A mole ratio is only as good as the coefficients it comes from. A skeleton equation, one with just the formulas, makes every ratio look like 1 : 1, and that’s almost never right. Here’s the same question worked both ways:

How many moles of O2 are needed to make 4.00 mol H2O?

If a problem gives you a skeleton equation, balance it first, the same way you did in Unit 3. Change only the coefficients. Changing a subscript would change the substance itself.

Coefficients, not subscripts

Both kinds of numbers are in every equation, and only one kind goes into a mole ratio. The subscript 2 in O2 says each oxygen molecule has 2 atoms. That’s part of what O2 is, and it doesn’t change from one reaction to the next. The coefficient in front says how many of those molecules react. In 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, the coefficient of O2 is 1.

Common mistakes

Watch-outs

Flipping the mole ratio

N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3. How many moles of NH3 form from 6.00 mol H2? The substance you want goes on top.

6.00 × 3 ÷ 2 = 9.00 mol NH3
6.00 × 2 ÷ 3 = 4.00 mol NH3

Using a subscript

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. The 2 in O2 counts atoms in one molecule. It isn’t a coefficient.

2 mol O2 : 2 mol H2O
1 mol O2 : 2 mol H2O

Skipping the balancing

The skeleton H2 + O2 → H2O makes every ratio 1 : 1. Balance it first.

1 mol O2 : 1 mol H2O
1 mol O2 : 2 mol H2O

Expecting the moles to add up

Atoms are conserved in a reaction. Molecules, and so moles, are not.

1 mol N2 + 3 mol H2 → 4 mol NH3
1 mol N2 + 3 mol H2 → 2 mol NH3

Reading coefficients as grams

Coefficients count particles, not mass. A mole of H2 and a mole of O2 have very different masses.

2 g H2 + 1 g O2 → 2 g H2O
2 mol H2 + 1 mol O2 → 2 mol H2O

Rounding to the coefficients

2Al + 3Cl2 → 2AlCl3, starting from 0.250 mol Al. Coefficients are exact, so keep the given amount’s 3 significant figures.

0.250 × 3 ÷ 2 = 0.4 mol Cl2
0.250 × 3 ÷ 2 = 0.375 mol Cl2
