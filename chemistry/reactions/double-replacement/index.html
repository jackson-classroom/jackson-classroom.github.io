Unit 3 · Double replacement

Partners swap, but something has to leave.

Two dissolved compounds trade partners. On paper that works for any pair. In a beaker it only counts as a reaction when the swap makes something that can't stay dissolved: a solid that falls out, or water. Your solubility chart tells you which.

Reacts. AgCl is insoluble, so it comes out of the solution as a white solid and settles to the bottom. That solid is the precipitate.

Recap

Swap partners, keep the charges

Double replacement starts with two ionic compounds, both dissolved in water. Each one is a cation and an anion. The two cations trade anions:

AB + CD → AD + CB

Ba2+Cl− + Na+SO42−
swap partners ↓
Ba2+SO42− + Na+Cl−

Writing the new formulas

  1. Split each reactant into its ions. Your Chem Ref sheet has the charges for the main groups and the polyatomic ions. For a transition metal, read the charge off the formula: FeCl3 has three Cl− ions, so the iron is Fe3+.
  2. Swap. The first cation takes the second anion, and the second cation takes the first anion. The cation is always written first.
  3. Build each formula from the charges, not from the old subscripts. Ba2+ and SO42− cancel one-to-one, so the formula is BaSO4. Na+ and Cl− cancel one-to-one, so it's NaCl.
  4. Keep polyatomic ions whole. If you need more than one, put it in parentheses: Ca(NO3)2, never CaNO32.
Nobody changes charge. In single replacement, a free element turns into an ion. In double replacement, every ion starts as an ion and stays the same ion. The Fe3+ in FeCl3 is still Fe3+ in the product:

Every swap makes two new compounds, and the solubility chart on your Chem Ref sheet tells you what each one does in water. Cations run down the side and anions run across the top. Each cell answers one question: if these two ions meet in water, does the compound stay dissolved?

The rule

Will it react?

A dissolved ionic compound isn't really sitting in the water as AgNO3. It's Ag+ ions and NO3− ions floating around separately. Mix two solutions and all you've done is put four kinds of ions in one beaker.

Something only happens if two of those ions grab each other and leave the solution. In this course that means one of two things:

  • A precipitate. One of the new compounds is I or P on your chart, so it comes out as a solid.
  • Water. H+ meets OH−. Once they're locked into water molecules, those ions are out of the solution too.

If both new compounds are S, every ion stays dissolved. The beaker has the same four ions it had before you mixed, so nothing happened. Write NR.

Only one product has to leave. In AgNO3 + NaCl, the NaNO3 stays dissolved, and that's normal. One precipitate is enough to make it a reaction.

H+ + OH−

Acids and hydroxides make water

An acid is a compound whose cation is H+. You'll see acids written as formulas that start with H, like HCl(aq), HNO3(aq) and H2SO4(aq). For double replacement, H+ is just another cation: split it off, swap partners, and use the H+ row on your chart.

Mix an acid with a hydroxide and the H+ ends up with the OH−. The H+ row, OH− column says HOH. That's water, H–O–H, and you write it as H2O(l).

The other product is a salt made from the metal ion and the acid's anion. Look it up like any other compound. In these problems it stays dissolved. Water forming is enough on its own, so an acid plus a hydroxide always reacts.

An acid can swap with a salt too

Picture it

Particle diagrams

A particle diagram zooms in on the beaker so you can see what an equation means. Each circle is one ion. The water the ions are dissolved in isn't drawn.

  • Dissolved, (aq): the ions are spread out on their own. A beaker of NaCl(aq) holds Na+ ions and Cl− ions floating separately, never NaCl pairs.
  • Precipitate, (s): the ions that found each other are packed together in a solid clump that settles to the bottom.
  • Water, (l): each H+ has joined an OH− to make one H2O molecule.
  • Spectators: ions that are floating on their own before mixing and are still floating on their own after. They're in the beaker the whole time but never change, which is why the net ionic equation leaves them out.

Ions only

Net ionic equations

Every equation so far is a molecular equation, with each compound written as one formula. But a dissolved compound is really separate ions. Writing it that way shows which ions actually do something.

Four steps

  1. Balance the molecular equation and put a state on every formula.
  2. Split every (aq) compound into its ions. The coefficient multiplies through: 2NaCl(aq) becomes 2Na+(aq) + 2Cl−(aq). Leave (s) and (l) alone, since a precipitate and water are not dissolved ions. This is the complete ionic equation.
  3. Cross out the spectators. These are ions that appear on both sides, unchanged. They were dissolved before and they're still dissolved after.
  4. Write what's left. That's the net ionic equation. If every coefficient divides by the same number, divide.

Common mistakes

Watch-outs

Carrying the old subscripts over

Na+ and Cl− cancel one-to-one, so the formula is NaCl. If you need two of them, that's a coefficient in front.

Breaking up a polyatomic ion

Two NO3− with one Ca2+ is Ca(NO3)2. Writing CaNO32 or CaN2O6 loses the nitrate. Keep the ion whole and wrap it in parentheses.

Treating P as dissolved

The Ca2+ row, OH− column says P. Partially soluble still counts as a precipitate, so it's (s), not (aq).

Writing products when everything is S

The formulas are right and it's balanced, but nothing left the solution. That's no reaction.

Splitting the precipitate

In a net ionic equation, only (aq) compounds get split into ions. A precipitate is a solid, so it stays as one formula: AgCl(s), never Ag+ + Cl− on the product side. Water stays H2O(l) for the same reason.

Changing a charge

FeCl3 means Fe3+, so with OH− it's Fe(OH)3, not Fe(OH)2. In double replacement every ion keeps the charge it came in with.
