Unit 3 · Nuclear reactions

The element can change. The numbers can't.

Every reaction so far kept its atoms: whatever went in came out, just with new partners. A nuclear reaction happens inside the nucleus, so one element can become another. What still has to balance is two numbers: the mass number on top and the atomic number on the bottom.

The basics

What radioactivity is

Every nucleus is a tight bundle of protons and neutrons. Most combinations hold together forever. Some don't: they have the wrong mix of protons and neutrons, or just too many of both. Those nuclei are unstable.

Sooner or later, an unstable nucleus settles down by throwing out a particle, a burst of energy, or both. That's radioactive decay, and whatever comes flying out is radiation. Nothing has to start it. Heating the sample, dissolving it or bonding it to something else won't speed it up or slow it down, because none of those reach the nucleus.

Chemical

Electrons and bonds

Atoms share or trade electrons and bond to new partners. Every atom comes through unchanged, so the same elements appear on both sides. You balance by counting atoms.

Nuclear

Inside the nucleus

Protons and neutrons are lost, gained or changed, so the element itself can change. The energy released is enormous by comparison. You balance by totaling mass numbers and atomic numbers.

The nucleus you start with is the parent. The new nucleus it turns into is the daughter.

How far radiation gets

The three most common kinds of radiation are stopped by different materials. The heavier and more charged the particle, the sooner it's stopped.

No state symbols. Nuclear equations don't carry (s), (l), (g) or (aq). The nucleus doesn't care whether its atom is in a solid, a liquid or a gas, so this is the one place in Unit 3 where states drop out.
Isotope notation

Reading a nuclear symbol

In a nuclear equation, every nucleus and every particle is written with two numbers stacked in front of its symbol.

Top · mass number
Protons plus neutrons. This uranium has 238 of them in total.
Bottom · atomic number
Protons only. This is what makes it uranium: every atom with 92 protons is uranium.
Neutrons · top minus bottom
238 − 92 = 146 neutrons.

An isotope is named by its element and its mass number: uranium-238 is the symbol above. The name gives you the top number. Get the bottom number from your periodic table.

The top number is a count, not the atomic mass. Your periodic table lists uranium's average atomic mass, 238.03. A nuclear symbol uses the whole-number mass number of one isotope: 238, or 235, or 234.

The particles

Six particles show up in nuclear equations. Each one gets a top and a bottom number, just like a nucleus. For a particle, the bottom number is its charge. That's what lets one rule balance every equation.

Alpha particle

A helium nucleus: 2 protons and 2 neutrons. Sometimes written with α instead of He.

Released: top −4, bottom −2
Beta particle

A fast electron. It forms when a neutron in the nucleus turns into a proton. Sometimes written with β.

Released: bottom +1
Positron

Like an electron, but with a +1 charge. It forms when a proton turns into a neutron.

Released: bottom −1
Proton

A hydrogen-1 nucleus. Also written with p instead of H.

Released: top −1, bottom −1
Neutron

No charge, so its bottom number is 0. Neutrons start fission and come out of it.

Released: top −1
Gamma ray

High-energy light. No mass and no charge, so it changes neither number.

Released: no change, energy leaves
Why does losing a beta particle raise the atomic number? The electron doesn't come from around the atom. A neutron inside the nucleus turns into a proton and an electron, and the electron is thrown out. One more proton means the atomic number goes up by 1. In the bookkeeping, a parent with a bottom number of 6 gives 6 = Z + (−1), so Z = 7.

Two rules

Balancing a nuclear equation

Total the top numbers on each side of the arrow. Then total the bottom numbers. In every nuclear reaction, both totals match.

Top

Mass numbers balance

Protons plus neutrons going in equals protons plus neutrons coming out. In the smoke detector, 241 = 237 + 4.

Bottom

Atomic numbers balance

The total charge stays the same. A −1 counts as −1: in carbon-14's decay, 6 = 7 + (−1).

That's all you need to fill in a missing piece. The missing piece has two unknown numbers, and you have two rules to find them.

  1. Set it up. Write the equation with a blank for the missing piece.
  2. Top. Total the top numbers on each side. The missing top number is whatever makes the two sides match.
  3. Bottom. Do the same with the bottom numbers. Watch the signs on −1 and +1.
  4. Name it. For a nucleus, look up the bottom number on your periodic table. For a particle, match both numbers to the chart above.

A number in front, like , means two of that particle. It multiplies both the top and the bottom number.

Worked example
Splitting and joining

Fission, fusion and bombardment

Decay happens on its own. These reactions don't: something has to hit the nucleus, or two nuclei have to be forced together. The bookkeeping is exactly the same.

Fission: a heavy nucleus splits

A neutron hits uranium-235, and it splits into two medium-sized nuclei, a few neutrons and a lot of energy. Those new neutrons can split more uranium nuclei, which keeps the reaction going. Nuclear power plants run on it.

Fusion: light nuclei join

Two very light nuclei are forced together and become one heavier nucleus, giving off a lot of energy. The Sun shines because hydrogen nuclei in its core fuse into helium.

Bombardment: hit it with a particle

Fire a particle at a nucleus and it can stick, knock something loose, or both. In 1919 Ernest Rutherford aimed alpha particles at nitrogen and got oxygen and a proton. It was the first time anyone turned one element into another on purpose. Hospitals use the same idea to make the radioactive isotopes for medical scans.

Count the coefficient. In the fission equation, means two neutrons. The top total on the right is 140 + 94 + 2 × 1 = 236, which matches 235 + 1 on the left. Forgetting the 2 is the most common fission mistake.

Keeping the old element

✓ Right

Getting the beta sign backwards

✗ Wrong
✓ Right
