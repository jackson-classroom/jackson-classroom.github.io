Unit 3 · Single replacement

A free element only takes the place of one that is weaker.

On paper, any element can trade places with any other. In a beaker, most of those swaps never happen. A ranked list, the activity series, tells you which ones do and when the honest answer is no reaction.

Recap

Single replacement in one line

A + BC → AC + B

One element starts out alone. We'll call it the free element. It meets a compound and takes the place of one of the elements in that compound, which is pushed out and ends up alone. There are two ways it happens in this course:

metal ⇄ metal

A metal replaces the cation

The free metal becomes a positive ion in the new compound, and the metal that was the cation comes out as the element. Hydrogen in an acid counts as a cation here, so a metal can replace it too.

halogen ⇄ halogen

A halogen replaces the anion

The free halogen becomes a negative ion, and the halide that was there comes out as a diatomic element. The sodium never moves.

Like replaces like. A metal goes after the cation, and a halogen goes after the anion. That tells you which two elements to compare, and comparing them is the whole question this page answers.

It still never happens. Drop a copper strip into zinc sulfate and you can watch it do nothing for a week. Balancing tells you whether atoms are conserved. It can't tell you whether the swap happens.
The rule

Above replaces below

The activity series ranks metals by how easily they give up electrons and become positive ions. The most active metal is at the top and the least active is at the bottom. A free metal replaces the metal in a compound only if the free metal is higher on the list. If it's lower, nothing happens, and you write NR for "no reaction."

Here's why it works that way. In , zinc atoms hand their electrons to copper ions. Zinc is the more active metal, so it would rather be the ion, and that trade goes downhill. Run it backward and you'd be asking copper to give electrons to zinc, which holds on to being an ion harder than copper does. It won't.

Use the order below. It's your class's list, the same one on your reference sheet. Some textbooks shuffle the top few metals, but for this class, this is the order.

How to read it

  1. Find the free element, the one written alone.
  2. Find the element it would replace: the metal (or hydrogen) in the compound for a free metal, the halide for a free halogen.
  3. If the free element is higher, it reacts. If it's lower, write NR. If it's the same element, there's nothing to swap.
Metals + acids

Hydrogen is on the list for a reason

Hydrogen is the only nonmetal on the metal list, and the dashed rung on the ladder shows why it's there. In an acid like HCl or H2SO4, hydrogen is the cation. A metal that sits above hydrogen can replace it. The products are a salt and hydrogen gas. The bubbles you see are H2 leaving.

Hydrogen is diatomic. When it leaves the acid, two H atoms pair up as H2, and that pairing is what puts the 2 in front of HCl in the zinc equation. Writing a lone H as a product is one of the most common mistakes on this topic.

Metals below hydrogen (Sb, Bi, Cu, W, Hg, Ag, Pt, Au) do not react with acids like HCl. That's a big part of why coins, wiring and jewelry are made from copper, silver and gold.

Why we don't drop sodium into copper sulfate solution in the lab. The metals at the very top of the list, lithium through sodium, are so active that they react with the water itself. They fizz, release hydrogen and sometimes catch fire. On paper the series says sodium beats copper, but in a beaker the sodium attacks the water before it ever reaches a copper ion. That's why the practice problems on this page start at magnesium.
Nonmetals

Halogens: read the column

Halogens aren't on the class list, and you don't need them to be. Their activity follows column 17 of the periodic table, from top to bottom:

F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2

For a halogen, being active means grabbing electrons to become a negative ion. Smaller atoms pull harder because the electron can get closer to the nucleus. Fluorine is the smallest, so it's the most active. A free halogen replaces any halide below it in the column and none above it.

Column 17 · click two rungs

Compare halogen with halogen. The metal ion in the salt (Na+, K+) is a bystander and has no say in whether the reaction happens. As for states, at room temperature F2 and Cl2 are gases, Br2 is a liquid and I2 is a solid: F2(g) Cl2(g) Br2(l) I2(s).

Same rule, opposite meaning. For metals, "active" means giving electrons away. For halogens, it means taking them. Either way, the one higher on its own list wins.
Writing products

Which ion does the metal form?

Once you know a metal reacts, it becomes an ion in the new compound, and you need its charge to write the formula. Some metals only ever form one ion. You're expected to know these:

Li+Na+K+ Mg2+Ca2+Sr2+Ba2+ Al3+Zn2+Cd2+Ag+

Most of the others can form more than one ion. When the free metal is one of these, the problem has to tell you which ion forms. It will do that in one of two ways:

  • It says so directly: "iron forms Fe3+ ions in this reaction."
  • It names the product with a Roman numeral: "…forming iron(III) sulfate." The (III) means Fe3+.

The recipe, start to finish

  1. Decide. Compare the free element with the one it would replace. If it's lower, write NR and stop.
  2. Make the ion. The free metal becomes a cation with its charge, or the free halogen becomes X−.
  3. Build the new compound. Pair that ion with the partner already in the compound, which is the same anion for a metal and the same cation for a halogen. Balance the charges with subscripts.
  4. Release the other element. A metal comes out as the element, as a solid (mercury is the one liquid metal). Hydrogen comes out as H2(g), and halogens come out diatomic.
  5. Balance with coefficients in lowest whole numbers.
  6. Add states. Solid metals are (s), dissolved ionic compounds and acids are (aq), H2 is (g), and halogens are F2(g), Cl2(g), Br2(l) and I2(s).
