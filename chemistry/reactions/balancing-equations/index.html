Conservation of mass

Why equations have to balance

A chemical reaction breaks bonds and makes new ones. That's all it does. The atoms themselves — the nuclei that decide whether something is carbon or oxygen — come through untouched. They are rearranged, not created or destroyed.

That's why mass is conserved. Burn methane in a sealed container and the container weighs the same afterward, because every atom you started with is still in there, just bonded to different partners. A balanced equation is the bookkeeping that proves it: each element appears the same number of times on both sides of the arrow.

Methane burning · balanced

Same 1 carbon, 4 hydrogen and 4 oxygen on both sides — just bonded to different partners.

Atoms are conserved. Molecules are not. N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g) starts with 4 molecules and ends with 2, and that's fine. What has to match is each element, counted separately: 2 N and 6 H on each side.
The one rule

Coefficients, never subscripts

Take the skeleton H2(g) + O2(g) → H2O(l). Oxygen is off — 2 on the left, 1 on the right. There are two ways you might try to get a second oxygen onto the right side. Only one of them still describes water.

Change the coefficient ✓
2H2O
2 molecules · 4 H · 2 O

Two separate water molecules. Still water — you just have more of it.

Change the subscript ✗
H2O2
1 molecule · 2 H · 2 O

Hydrogen peroxide — the antiseptic in the brown bottle. A different chemical with different properties. The equation now describes a reaction that isn't the one you were asked about.

2H2O

Coefficient — the big number in front

Counts how many molecules or formula units. This is the only number you change while balancing.

H2O

Subscript — the small number inside

Part of the substance's identity. It was fixed when you wrote the skeleton from charges or prefixes, and it stays fixed.

The legal fix for the water equation is coefficients only: 2H2(g) + O2(g) → 2H2O(l). Four H and two O on each side. If a formula looks wrong while you're balancing, the problem is in the skeleton — go back to Writing Equations and fix it there.

Before you balance

Counting atoms

You can't balance what you can't count. Three rules cover every formula you'll see:

  • A subscript belongs to the symbol right before it. In CO2 the 2 is on the oxygen only. No subscript means 1.
  • A subscript after parentheses multiplies everything inside. Ca(NO3)2 has two whole nitrate groups: 2 N and 6 O.
  • A coefficient multiplies everything in the formula. Not just the first element — every atom, including the ones inside parentheses.

Order of attack

A strategy that works

Balancing is trial and error, but it doesn't have to be random. Working in a smart order means each new coefficient rarely undoes one you already set.

1

Write the correct skeleton first

Right formulas, diatomics as H2, O2, Cl2 and so on, states included. No amount of balancing fixes a wrong formula.

2

Keep polyatomic ions whole

If SO4, NO3, PO4 or OH shows up unchanged on both sides, count it as one unit instead of splitting it into atoms.

3

Start with the most complex formula

Pick an element from the biggest formula. Leave anything that appears alone — O2, H2, a lone metal — for last, since changing it affects nothing else.

4

Combustion: C, then H, then O

Carbon into CO2, hydrogen into H2O, oxygen last. If O2 needs a half, double every coefficient.

5

Check every element, then reduce

Recount each element on both sides. If every coefficient shares a factor, divide it out — answers go in lowest whole numbers.
