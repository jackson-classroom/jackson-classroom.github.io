Unit 3 · Half-life

Half of what's left, every time.

A radioactive sample doesn't run out on a schedule. In each half-life, half of the nuclei that are still there decay. So the amount goes 64 → 32 → 16 → 8, getting smaller and smaller but never all gone at once.

The idea

What half-life means

The half-life of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for half of the nuclei in a sample to decay. Every isotope has its own, and they range from a fraction of a second to billions of years.

IsotopeHalf-lifeWhere you'd meet it
Fluorine-18110 minutesPET scans
Technetium-99m6.0 hoursHospital imaging scans
Radon-2223.8 daysA gas that seeps into basements
Cobalt-605.3 yearsCancer radiation therapy
Carbon-145730 yearsDating ancient wood and bone
Uranium-2384.5 billion yearsDating the oldest rocks on Earth

The decayed nuclei don't vanish. Each one has turned into its daughter nuclide, the product from its nuclear equation. Half-life only tracks how much of the original radioactive isotope is left.

Random for one, predictable for many

Nobody can say when one particular nucleus will decay. Each one just has a 50% chance of decaying during the next half-life, a lot like a coin toss. With thousands of nuclei, though, very close to half of them decay every half-life. Try it: every dot below is one radioactive nucleus.

The method

The halving table

Every half-life problem here works out to a whole number of half-lives, so you never need a formula. You need a table with one row per half-life.

  1. Start the table. Row 0 is time 0 and the amount you start with.
  2. Count the half-lives. Number of half-lives = total time ÷ half-life.
  3. Halve, one row at a time. Each row adds one half-life to the time and cuts the amount in half.
  4. Read the answer from the row the question asks about.

Five kinds of questions

Every half-life question gives you some of the table and asks for the rest. Work out which piece is missing, and you know what to do.

1

How much is left?

Count the half-lives (time ÷ half-life), then halve the starting amount that many times.

48 g, 3 half-lives: 48 → 24 → 12 → 6 g
2

What fraction is left?

Same as #1, but start from 1. Each half-life doubles the bottom of the fraction.

4 half-lives: 1 → 1/2 → 1/4 → 1/8 → 1/16
3

How long did it take?

Halve until you reach the amount that's left. Count the halvings, then multiply by the half-life.

1/8 left = 3 halvings; 3 × 2.7 days = 8.1 days
4

What's the half-life?

Halve until you reach the amount that's left. Count the halvings, then divide the total time by that count.

80 → 40 → 20 → 10 in 12 days: 12 ÷ 3 = 4 days
5

How much was there at the start?

Count the half-lives, then double backward from the amount left, once per half-life.

5 g left after 2 half-lives: 5 → 10 → 20 g
Common mistakes

Watch-outs

"Two half-lives and it's all gone"

Half plus half sounds like a whole, but the second half-life only removes half of what's left. After two half-lives, a quarter of the sample is still there.

100 g → 50 g → 0 g
100 g → 50 g → 25 g

Dividing by the number of half-lives

Three half-lives doesn't mean "divide by 3." It means halve three times.

160 g ÷ 3 = 53 g
160 → 80 → 40 → 20 g

Halving three times isn't ÷ 6

÷ 2 ÷ 2 ÷ 2 is ÷ 8. When in doubt, write out the table instead of shortcutting.

Counting row 0

Row 0 is the start, before any time passes. If your last row is row 4, that's 4 half-lives, not 5.

Stopping at the number of half-lives

If the question asks how long, "3" isn't a time. Multiply by the half-life: 3 × 5.2 days = 15.6 days.

Removing the same amount each time

80 → 40 removes 40 g, but 40 → 20 removes only 20 g. Each half-life takes half of what's left, so less and less decays.
