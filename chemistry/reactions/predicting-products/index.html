The method

Four questions for any reaction

Keep your Chem Ref sheet out. You'll use its charges, polyatomic ions, activity series and solubility chart. Then work through the same four questions every time, in this order:

  1. What type is it? Read the reactants only.One compound alone · a C–H fuel with O2 · an element with a compound · two ionic compounds · two elementsType
  2. What would form? Use that type's recipe and build every ionic formula from the charges.Free H, N, O, F, Cl, Br and I come out diatomic. Polyatomic ions stay whole.Formulas
  3. Does it actually happen? Only the two replacement types can fail.Single replacement: the activity series. Double replacement: the solubility chart.React or NR
  4. Where does each product end up? Put one state after every product formula.(s), (l), (g) or (aq)States
Rusty on a step? Naming the type and the product recipes are on Types of Reactions (page 3). The series is on The Activity Series (page 4), and reading the chart is on Double Replacement (page 5).
Question 3

Does it actually happen?

Writing the products only tells you what would form. Before you add states, decide whether the reaction goes at all. If it doesn't, the whole answer is NR.

Above replaces below

Single replacement

Find both elements on the activity series. A metal only replaces a metal (or the hydrogen in an acid like HCl) that sits below it. A halogen only replaces a halogen below it in column 17: F2, Cl2, Br2, I2.

Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)
Cu(s) + ZnSO4(aq) → NR
Something has to leave the solution

Double replacement

Swap partners, then look up both new compounds on your chart: cation's row, anion's column. If either one is I or P, a solid precipitate forms. If H+ meets OH−, water forms. If both are S, nothing changes: NR.

BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) → BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)
KNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) → NR
These always go

Combination, decomposition, combustion

In this class there is no NR check for these three. Write the products and move on to states.

2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s)
CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)
Question 4

Adding states

Every product gets exactly one state. Most of them you can decide without looking anything up. The only lookup is for an ionic compound formed in water, and that's what the solubility chart on your Chem Ref sheet is for.

ProductStateHow you know
A free metal: Cu, Ag, Zn, Na…(s)Metals are solids at room temperature.
Mercury, Hg(l)The one liquid metal.
H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2(g)Gases at room temperature. Bubbles of H2 are what you see when a metal reacts with an acid.
Bromine, Br2(l)The one liquid nonmetal. When it is pushed out into water, (aq) is accepted too.
Iodine, I2(s)A solid at room temperature. When it is pushed out into water, (aq) is accepted too.
An ionic compound made from two elements (combination)(s)There's no water around to dissolve it.
An ionic compound made in water (single or double replacement)(aq) or (s)Look it up: cation's row, anion's column. S means it stays dissolved, (aq). I or P means it's a precipitate, (s).
Water made when H+ meets OH−(l)Water is the liquid everything else is dissolved in.
CO2 and H2O from combustion(g)Both leave the flame as hot gases. H2O(l) is accepted too.

(aq) means "dissolved in water." Only an ionic compound or an acid that's in solution gets it. A free metal is never (aq).

The state of a free element comes from the periodic table, so in the practice below a slip on an element's state still counts. The feedback tells you the usual state.

Common mistakes

Watch-outs

NR means there's nothing to write

If the series or the chart says no, the answer is just NR. Don't write the products that would have formed.

Cu(s) + ZnSO4(aq) →notCuSO4(aq) + Zn(s)writeNR

Two soluble products is still NR

In double replacement, if both new compounds are S on your chart, the four ions just stay mixed in the water. Writing them all as (aq) describes the same mixture you started with.

KNO3 + NaCl →notKCl(aq) + NaNO3(aq)writeNR

A metal that comes out is (s), never (aq)

In single replacement the element that gets pushed out leaves as the free element. Only a dissolved ionic compound gets (aq).

notCu(aq)writeCu(s)

Precipitates are (s); water is (l)

A precipitate is a solid, so write (s), not (ppt). P on the chart counts as a precipitate too. Water from H+ and OH− is the liquid itself, not something dissolved in it.

notAgCl(ppt)writeAgCl(s)notH2O(aq)writeH2O(l)
