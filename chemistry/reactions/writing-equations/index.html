The anatomy of an equation

A chemical equation describes a reaction in symbols. The substances you start with, the reactants, go on the left. The substances that form, the products, go on the right. The arrow between them reads "yields" or "produces."

Translating a sentence comes down to spotting signal words. Some phrases mean "these meet each other" and become a plus sign. Others mean "and this is what comes out" and become the arrow. Everything else names a substance and tells you its state.

+

Joins substances on the same side

Read it as "and" or "reacts with."

reacts withandis added tois mixed withis placed inis burned in oxygen
→

Separates start from finish

Read it as "yields," "produces," or "forms."

producesyieldsformscombine to formdecomposes intoproducing

One phrase does double duty. "Is burned in oxygen" is a connector and a reactant at once: it means + O2(g) on the left, even though the sentence never says "oxygen gas."

Conditions stay in the sentence. "When heated," "an electric current," and "is bubbled through" tell you how a reaction happens, not what is in it. In this class nothing is ever written over the arrow (no Δ, no catalysts), and heat is never written as a reactant.
These are skeleton equations. A skeleton has the right formulas, the right states, and everything on the right side of the arrow, but it is not balanced yet. If you feel the urge to put a 2 in front of something, hold that thought. Balancing is the next page.
Read the wording

State symbols come from the words

Every species in an equation gets a state symbol in parentheses right after its formula. You don't guess it from memory. The sentence tells you, and every sentence on this page says each state outright.

SymbolMeansWords that signal it
(s)solid"solid," "a strip of," "powdered," "crystals"
(l)liquid"liquid" — a pure liquid such as water, bromine, mercury
(g)gas"gas," "gaseous," "vapor"; "burned in oxygen" means O2(g)
(aq)aqueous"aqueous," "a solution of," "an aqueous solution of," "dissolved in water"

(aq) is not (l). Aqueous means dissolved in water. The substance itself isn't a liquid; it is spread out among water molecules. Salt water is NaCl(aq), never NaCl(l). Water on its own is a pure liquid, H2O(l), and water vapor is H2O(g).

Alone vs. in a compound

Seven elements come in pairs

Seven elements never exist as single atoms on their own. Left alone, they pair up into two-atom molecules:

H2hydrogenN2nitrogenO2oxygenF2fluorineCl2chlorineBr2bromineI2iodine

To find them on the periodic table, start at nitrogen, element 7. Nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, then down through chlorine, bromine, and iodine trace the shape of a 7. Add hydrogen, sitting off by itself, and you have all seven.

The 2 only applies when the element stands alone: "chlorine gas," "liquid bromine," "hydrogen gas," "burned in oxygen." Once the element is inside a compound it is bonded to something else, and the subscripts come from charges or prefixes instead.

Cl2(g)

Alone: always the pair

"Chlorine gas" is the molecule Cl2. Writing Cl, or 2 Cl, describes loose atoms that don't exist on their own.

NaCl · CaCl2

In a compound: charges decide

Na+ and Cl− cancel one to one: NaCl, not NaCl2. CaCl2 has a 2 because Ca2+ needs two chlorides, not because chlorine is diatomic.

Unit 2 refresher

Names to formulas, quickly

You already know how to do this. Here is the short version, so the formulas in the equations come out right. Use your reference sheet for ion charges and polyatomic ions.

Al3+ O2− → Al2O3

Ionic: make the charges cancel

Criss-cross the charge numbers into subscripts, then reduce. Ca2+ and O2− give CaO, not Ca2O2.

iron(III) = Fe3+

Roman numerals: the metal's charge

Iron(III) chloride is FeCl3; iron(II) chloride is FeCl2. Copper(II) sulfate is CuSO4, because 2+ and 2− already cancel.

Ca(NO3)2

Polyatomic ions stay in one piece

When you need more than one, wrap it in parentheses: Ca(NO3)2, (NH4)3PO4. With just one, no parentheses: NaNO3.

N2O5

Covalent: prefixes are subscripts

Di-nitrogen pent-oxide is N2O5. Carbon dioxide is CO2. "Mono" is dropped on the first element.

Names you just have to know

Water is H2O, hydrogen peroxide is H2O2, ammonia is NH3, and methane is CH4. Other fuels on this page, like propane and octane, come with their formula in the sentence. So do the two acids, hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4).

Read before you practice

Six ways to lose the point

Almost every wrong skeleton equation comes from one of these. The practice checker below looks for each one by name.

Forgetting a diatomic

WrongNa(s) + Cl(g)
RightNa(s) + Cl2(g)

"Chlorine gas" is the element alone, so it's the pair.

Writing "oxygen" as O

WrongCH4(g) + O(g)
RightCH4(g) + O2(g)

Oxygen you burn things in is always O2(g).

Carrying the 2 into a compound

WrongNaCl2(s)
RightNaCl(s)

Inside a compound, chlorine is chloride, Cl−. Charges set the subscripts.

Picking the wrong state

WrongNaCl(l)
RightNaCl(aq)

"Aqueous" means dissolved in water, which is (aq). Read the state from the sentence every time.

Changing subscripts to make it "fit"

WrongH2 + O2 → H2O2
RightH2 + O2 → H2O

H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. Subscripts are fixed by the name. Balancing uses coefficients, and that is next page.

Dropping the parentheses

WrongCaNO32
RightCa(NO3)2

Without parentheses, "NO32" reads as 32 oxygens.

Don't balance. If you do anyway, that's fine. A correct skeleton with coefficients still counts.
