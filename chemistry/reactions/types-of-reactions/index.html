The five patterns

A chemical equation is a before-and-after picture. Atoms are never created or destroyed, so the only thing that can change is who is bonded to whom. There are only a handful of ways to reshuffle partners, and this course uses five of them.

Each card gives the general form with letters standing in for atoms or ions, and one real example.

A + B → AB

Combination (synthesis)

Two or more reactants join into one product. Usually two elements; sometimes two simple compounds.

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO
AB → A + B

Decomposition

One compound breaks into simpler pieces, often all the way to its elements.

2H2O → 2H2 + O2
A + BC → AC + Ba metal replaces the metalA + BC → BA + Ca halogen replaces the halogen

Single replacement

A lone element swaps with the matching part of a compound. A metal pushes out the metal (the front of the formula); a halogen pushes out the halogen (the back).

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu
AB + CD → AD + CB

Double replacement

Two ionic compounds swap partners. Each cation stays in front and picks up the other compound's anion.

AgNO3 + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO3
CxHy(Oz) + O2 → CO2 + H2O

Combustion

A fuel made of carbon and hydrogen (alcohols and sugars also contain oxygen) burns in O2. The products are always CO2 and H2O.

CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O
Element or compound? A formula with only one kind of atom is an element, even with a subscript: Zn, O2, Cl2, and Br2 are all elements. A formula with two or more kinds of atoms (NaCl, H2O, CuSO4) is a compound. Almost every decision on this page starts with that question.

Spot the type from the reactants

You do not need the products to name the type. The reactants give it away. Ask these questions in order and stop at the first yes:

  1. Is there only one reactant, a compound sitting alone?2HgO → … · CaCO3 → …Decomposition
  2. Is O2 reacting with a fuel that contains C and H?C3H8 + O2 → … · C2H5OH + O2 → …Combustion
  3. Is one reactant a free element and the other a compound?Mg + HCl → … · Cl2 + NaBr → …Single replacement
  4. Are there two ionic compounds, usually both (aq)?BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) → …Double replacement
  5. Are there two elements, or two small compounds that join into one product?Al + Br2 → … · CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2Combination

The order matters in one place. CH4 + O2 is technically "an element plus a compound," but it is a fuel burning in oxygen, so it is combustion, not single replacement. Asking the combustion question first keeps you out of that trap.

The other trap is two compounds that do not trade anything. CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2 starts with two compounds, but they join into one product. One product means combination.

Writing the products

Once you know the type, the products follow a recipe. In every type that makes an ionic compound, the subscripts come from the charges, never from the reactant formulas. Use your reference sheet for charges and polyatomic ions. Write the formulas first, then balance with coefficients.

Metal + nonmetal → one ionic compound. The metal becomes a cation, the nonmetal becomes an anion, and the charges decide the ratio.

ReactantsIonsProductBalanced
Mg + O2Mg2+, O2−MgO2Mg + O2 → 2MgO
Al + Br2Al3+, Br−AlBr32Al + 3Br2 → 2AlBr3
Li + N2Li+, N3−Li3N6Li + N2 → 2Li3N

Notice that the 2 in Br2 disappears inside the product. It describes free bromine only. Once bromine is an ion in a compound, the charges set its subscript.

Two small compounds can also combine, such as SO3 + H2O → H2SO4 or CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2. You only need to recognize those; you won't be asked to predict them yet.

Binary compound → its two elements. Write each element the way it exists on its own: metals as single atoms, and the seven diatomics (H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2, I2) in pairs.

ReactantElements releasedBalanced
H2OH2 + O22H2O → 2H2 + O2
NaClNa + Cl22NaCl → 2Na + Cl2
HgOHg + O22HgO → 2Hg + O2
Not every decomposition goes to elements. Some compounds break into smaller compounds: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2, and 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2. These are still decomposition, because one reactant falls apart. For now you only predict products for the binary kind.

The lone element takes the place of its match. A metal replaces the metal (or the hydrogen in an acid); a halogen replaces the halogen. Then rebuild the new compound from charges.

ReactantsWhat swapsBalanced
Zn + CuSO4Zn2+ takes Cu2+'s place; Cu comes out freeZn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu
Al + CuCl2Al3+ needs three Cl−: AlCl32Al + 3CuCl2 → 2AlCl3 + 3Cu
Mg + HClMg replaces hydrogen; H comes out as H2Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2
Cl2 + NaBrCl− takes Br−'s place; Br comes out as Br2Cl2 + 2NaBr → 2NaCl + Br2
The pattern is not a promise. Writing the products only tells you what would form. Whether a single replacement actually happens depends on whether the lone element is more active than the one it is trying to push out. That is the job of the activity series, on the next page. Every single replacement on this page is one that does happen.

Split, swap, rebuild. Split each compound into its cation and anion. Pair the first cation with the second anion and the second cation with the first anion. Rebuild each formula from charges, cation first.

ReactantsIonsProducts
CaCl2 + Na2CO3Ca2+ Cl− | Na+ CO32−CaCO3 + NaCl
Ca(NO3)2 + Na3PO4Ca2+ NO3− | Na+ PO43−Ca3(PO4)2 + NaNO3

Balanced, the second one is 3Ca(NO3)2 + 2Na3PO4 → Ca3(PO4)2 + 6NaNO3. Phosphate stays a single unit the whole way through, and when you need more than one of it, it goes in parentheses.

Which of these swaps actually produce a visible change (a solid forming out of solution) is next week's topic. For now you are predicting formulas only.

Fuel + O2 → CO2 + H2O, every time. All the carbon ends up in CO2 and all the hydrogen in H2O. Any oxygen in the fuel itself just joins the oxygen pool.

FuelBalanced
Methane, CH4CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O
Ethane, C2H62C2H6 + 7O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O
Ethanol, C2H5OH (C2H6O)C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O
Glucose, C6H12O6C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O

Balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen last, since O2 is the only reactant that is a single element. If oxygen comes out odd, as it does for ethane, double everything.

Common mistakes

Watch-outs

A metal burning in oxygen is combination

Magnesium burns with a blinding flame, but 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO has two elements making one product. In this class, combustion means a fuel containing carbon and hydrogen (sometimes oxygen too) reacting with O2 to make CO2 + H2O. No C–H fuel, no combustion.

Mg + O2 → combinationCH4 + O2 → combustion

Charges set the subscripts, not the reactants

Na+ and Cl− cancel one-to-one, so sodium and chlorine make NaCl. The 2 in Cl2 describes free chlorine only; it does not carry into the compound.

notNaCl2writeNaCl

Free diatomics come out in pairs

When decomposition or single replacement releases H, N, O, F, Cl, Br, or I as a free element, it leaves as a diatomic molecule. Metals never do: sodium on its own is Na.

notNa + ClnotNa2 + Cl2writeNa + Cl2

In double replacement, swap partners and keep ions whole

Cations stay in front; only the anions trade places. Polyatomic ions such as SO42− travel as one unit and never get broken up or merged with another ion.

BaCl2 + Na2SO4 →notBaNa + Cl2SO4writeBaSO4 + NaCl

Predict the products

You get the reactants; you type the products. Formulas only: no states, and you don't need to balance. Separate products with a plus sign, in any order, and type subscripts as plain digits (Na2SO4, Ca(OH)2).
