2.6.A

Place and marketing channels

Place

Where and how customers get access to a product. It’s the “P” of the marketing mix that comes after product and price.

The same product can be sold in very different places:

Retail stores

Stores that sell many brands, like a grocery store or a sporting goods chain.

Company-owned stores

A brand’s own shops, where it sells only its own products.

Club memberships

Warehouse clubs that only members can shop at, or monthly subscription clubs.

Online

A company’s own website or app, or a big online marketplace.

Marketing channel (distribution channel)

All of the individuals and businesses required to deliver a finished product to the final customer. A business’s marketing channels determine its place.

A marketing channel is the final stage of a supply chain. Everything before it is about making the product; the channel is about getting the finished product to the person who buys it.

Stage 1Raw materials are gathered
Stage 2Parts are made
Stage 3The product is finished
Final stage · marketing channelThe finished product reaches the customer

Who is the customer? B2C and B2B

B2C · business-to-consumer

Consumer products

Sold to people for their own use: snacks, phones, shoes. Typical channels are websites and retail stores.

B2B · business-to-business

Business products

Sold to other businesses to run their operations or make their own products: steel beams, cash registers, cleaning supplies for hospitals. A typical channel is an industrial distributor.

The same company can use both. A paper company might sell notebooks to shoppers through stores (B2C) and printer paper to offices through a distributor (B2B).

Direct or indirect?

Channels also differ in how many businesses stand between the producer and the final customer. Those in-between businesses are called intermediaries: wholesalers, distributors, and retailers.

Direct

No intermediaries

Producer→Customer

A bakery sells at its own counter. A shoe brand sells through its own website and its own stores.

Indirect

One intermediary

Producer→Retailer→Customer

A small hot sauce maker sells cases to a regional grocery chain, which sells bottles to shoppers.

Indirect

Two or more intermediaries

Producer→Wholesaler→Retailer→Customer

A candy maker sells truckloads to a wholesaler, which supplies thousands of gas stations and corner stores.

B2B · indirect

Through an industrial distributor

Producer→Industrial distributor→Business customer

A bolt factory sells to a distributor, which stocks construction companies across the state.

Every intermediary gets paid. Each one buys the product for less than it sells it for. The more steps in the channel, the smaller the share of the final price the producer keeps. You’ll see this in the channel lab below.

2.6.B

Choosing a marketing channel

There’s no single best channel. A business picks one, or a mix, by comparing each option on three things:

1 · Cost and profit

What does it cost, and what’s left?

Setting up the channel, running it, and the share intermediaries keep, compared with how much profit it can bring in.

2 · Customer experience

What happens when they buy?

How the product is shown, explained, priced, and supported, and who controls all of that.

3 · Reach

Can it get to the target customers?

Whether the channel delivers the product to the people the business is trying to sell to, and how many of them.

The trade-off

ControlKeeps control over pricing and the customer experienceIntermediaries decide the shelf price, where the product sits, and how it’s presented

As a shopper

The channel changes what you pay and who helps you after you buy. The same speaker might cost less on a big online marketplace than on the brand’s own site, but if it breaks, you may have to deal with the third-party seller, not the brand. Before you buy, check who is actually selling it and whose return policy and warranty you’ll get.
