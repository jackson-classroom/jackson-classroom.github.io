Market Research

Every new product starts as a guess: people will want this, we can make it, and it will make money. Market research is how a business checks those guesses before it bets real money on them.

2.3.A

Why research before you build

2.3.A.1
Market research

Gathering and analyzing information about customers, competitors, and the market so a business can make better decisions.

Making a product, stocking it, and advertising it all cost money. Research costs much less than a flop. It answers questions like: Is there a real problem here? Who has it? What would they pay? Who else is already solving it?

Two kinds of research data

Research produces two kinds of data (2.3.A.2). The quickest test: is it a number, or is it words?

Quantitative
Numbers: anything you can count, measure, or put on a chart.
percentages1–5 ratingssales countspricesclick rates

Answers how many, how much, how often: “62% of students skip breakfast at least twice a week.”

Qualitative
Words, descriptions, and observations: what people say and do, in their own terms.
interview quotesopen-ended answerswritten reviewsobserver notes

Answers why and how it feels: “I skip breakfast because my bus comes at 6:50.”

Businesses usually want both. The numbers show how big a pattern is; the words explain why it happens.

Watch-out: the method doesn’t decide the type of data. One survey can collect both: a 1–5 rating is quantitative, and the comment box under it is qualitative. Look at the data itself.

Three questions before launch

Before a business brings a new product to market, research helps it answer three questions (2.3.A.3). A product needs a yes on all three.

Desirable · Do customers want it?

Is there a real need or want, and would people choose this over what they use now?

Feasible · Can we make and deliver it?

Can the business get the materials, skills, technology, and permits it needs?

Viable · Can it make money?

Will customers pay more than it costs to make, sell, and deliver, often enough to keep the business going?

2.3.B

Ask people, or look it up?

Every piece of research has a source. The question is who collected it, and for what.

2.3.B.1
Primary research
New data the business collects itself, for its own question.
surveysinterviewsfocus groupsobservationsexperimentsA/B tests
  • Answers exactly your question, about your customers, right now.
  • Costs more time and money to collect.
2.3.B.2
Secondary research
Data someone else already collected, for some other purpose.
government data (Census, labor statistics)industry reportscompetitors’ websites and pricesnews articlespublished studies
  • Cheaper and faster: it already exists.
  • Wasn’t built for your question, and it may be out of date.
Start with secondary. It’s usually the cheaper way to size up the market and your rivals (2.3.B.3): how many potential customers live nearby, what competitors charge, whether the industry is growing. Then spend money on primary research for what only your own customers can tell you.

Quick test: did the business collect it for this question? Primary. Did the business find it already collected? Secondary, even if the business paid for it.

2.3.C

Testing a hypothesis about customers

Business hypothesis

A specific, testable guess about customers: “We think [these customers] will [do this] because [reason].”

Primary research turns a guess into evidence. The best hypotheses include something you can check. “At least 40% of students would buy one every week” can be proven wrong. “Students will like it” can’t.

Six ways to collect primary research

The course framework names six methods (2.3.C.1). Each is good at something different.

Survey

The same questions to many people, on paper, online, or in person.

Gives youmostly numbers
Watch out: people don’t always do what they say they’ll do.
Interview

A one-on-one conversation with follow-up questions.

Gives youmostly words
Watch out: it takes time, so you only reach a few people.
Focus group

A small group discusses a product together, guided by a moderator.

Gives youmostly words
Watch out: one loud voice can sway the whole group.
Observation

Watch what people actually do, in a store, online, or wherever they use the product.

Gives youboth
Watch out: it shows what people do, but not why.
Experiment

Change one thing, like a price, a display, or an offer, and measure what happens.

Gives younumbers
Watch out: change more than one thing and you can’t tell which one caused the result.
A/B test

Show version A to one group and version B to a similar group at the same time, then compare.

Gives younumbers
Watch out: it needs enough people for the difference to mean something.

2.3.C

Survey lab: can you trust the answer?

A business almost never asks every customer. It asks a sample: a smaller group that’s supposed to stand in for everyone. Whether a sample tells the truth depends on two things:

Who you ask

Biased vs. random

A biased sample leans toward one kind of person, like your friends or a brand’s fans. In a random sample, everyone has the same chance of being picked.

How many you ask

Small samples bounce

Ask 10 people and each answer moves the result by 10 points. Ask more people and the result settles down.

Survey lab · Ridgeview High, 400 students
Blend Bus’s hypothesisAt least 40% of Ridgeview students would buy a $5 smoothie before school at least once a week.

Blend Bus, a made-up smoothie truck, will only park outside Ridgeview High before first bell if the hypothesis holds. You can’t ask all 400 students, so choose who to ask and how many, then run the survey.

The takeaway: ask the right people, and enough of them. A bigger sample fixes the bouncing, but it doesn’t fix bias. Asking all 30 of your friends just gives you the same wrong answer every time.

Supported, not supported, or can’t tell?

The last step is reading the results honestly. Sometimes the honest answer is that the research can’t tell you yet: too few people, the wrong people, more than one change at a time, or a measure that doesn’t match the hypothesis.

As a shopper

Ads quote research too: “9 out of 10 users love it,” “rated #1 by customers.” Run the same checks you used in the lab:

  • Who was asked? Customers who already bought it, the company’s own employees, or a random sample?
  • How many? 10 people or 10,000?
  • Who paid for it? A study paid for by the company selling the product deserves a second look.
