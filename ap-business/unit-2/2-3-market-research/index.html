Market Research

Every new product starts as a guess: people will want this, we can make it, and it will make money. Market research is how a business checks those guesses before it bets real money on them.

2.3.A

Why businesses research first

2.3.A.1
Market research

The process of collecting detailed information about markets, products, and customer behavior to guide marketing decisions.

Making a product, stocking it, and advertising it all cost money. Research costs far less than a flop. It answers questions like: Is there a real problem here? Who has it? What would they pay? Who else is already solving it?

Two kinds of research data

Market researchers collect both kinds (2.3.A.2). The quickest test: is it a number, or is it words and images?

2.3.A.2.i
Quantitative
Numerical data that can be measured and analyzed.
percentages1–5 ratingssales countspricesclick rates

Answers how many, how much, and how often: “62% of students skip breakfast at least twice a week.”

2.3.A.2.ii
Qualitative
Descriptive, non-numerical data, collected in words and images.
interview quotesopen-ended answerswritten reviewsobserver notesphotos and video

Answers why and how: “I skip breakfast because my bus comes at 6:50.”

Businesses usually want both. The numbers show how big a pattern is; the words explain why it happens.

Watch-out: the method doesn’t decide the type of data. One survey can collect both: a 1–5 rating is quantitative, and the comment box under it is qualitative. Look at the data itself.

Desirable, feasible, viable

Businesses developing new products use market research to evaluate three things before committing significant resources to bring the product to market (2.3.A.3). A product needs a yes on all three.

Desirable · Does it create value for customers?

It achieves problem-solution fit: it solves a problem customers really have, in a way they value. (2.3.A.3.i)

Feasible · Can we produce and provide it?

The business has the capacity to make it within the limits of its resources, technology, expertise, and time. (2.3.A.3.ii)

Viable · Can it be profitable?

It has the potential to be profitable in a market. (2.3.A.3.iii)

Research doesn’t stop after launch

Businesses with existing products keep researching (2.3.A.4). They use it to:

Changing needs and wants

Keep up with customers

A cereal brand learns that more families want less sugar.

Product innovation

Find the next product

Reviews keep asking for a bigger size, so the brand makes one.

Market share

Keep or win customers

A rival is winning younger buyers, so the brand rethinks its ads.

2.3.B

What’s already out there? Secondary research

Businesses use two kinds of research to learn about customers, competitors, and the market landscape (2.3.B.1). The difference is who collected it, and for what.

2.3.C.1
Primary research
New data the business collects itself, for its own question.
surveysinterviewsfocus groupsobservationsexperimentsA/B tests
  • Answers exactly your question, about your customers, right now.
  • Costs more time and money to collect.
2.3.B.2
Secondary research
Information someone else already gathered, from external sources.
government publications and databasescommercial reportsacademic studiesrivals’ websites and pricesnews articles
  • More cost-effective: it already exists.
  • Wasn’t built for your question, and it may be out of date.
Start with secondary. Businesses typically study secondary research, including detailed information about rival businesses, to size up the market landscape for a product idea, because it’s more cost-effective than running extensive primary research (2.3.B.3). Then they spend money on primary research for what only their own customers can tell them.

Quick test: did the business collect it for this question? Primary. Did the business find it already collected? Secondary, even if the business paid for it.

What secondary research can tell you

Secondary sources often cover four things that help a business judge whether its product could be viable (2.3.B.2):

Market size

How big is it?

Measured in dollars and in total customers.

Market trends

Which way is it moving?

Growing, shrinking, or shifting toward something new.

Market segments

Who buys?

The groups that make up the market, and how big each one is.

Customer decisions

What drives the choice?

The factors that influence what customers buy.

Worked example · sizing a market

Census data shows about 52,000 teens ages 13–17 in the county. An industry report says teens spend about $180 a year on sneakers. So the market is roughly 52,000 customers, and 52,000 × $180 ≈ $9.4 million a year. A new sneaker shop can use that to judge whether there’s room for one more seller.

PESTEL: the forces outside the business

Research also covers the PESTEL factors that shape a market opportunity (2.3.B.1): six big forces a business can’t control but has to plan around.

P
Political

What the government is doing: policies, spending, elections, stability.

A city funds new bike lanes.
E
Economic

The health of the economy: incomes, jobs, prices, interest rates.

Interest rates rise, so car loans cost more.
S
Social

Population, culture, lifestyles, and what people value.

More teens choose plant-based diets.
T
Technological

New tools and inventions that change products or how people buy.

Tap-to-pay spreads everywhere.
E
Environmental

Weather, climate, and natural resources.

Hotter summers shorten ski season.
L
Legal

Specific laws and regulations the business must follow.

A law bans energy-drink sales to minors.

Political vs. legal trips people up. Political is what the government is doing or planning. Legal is the specific rules already on the books that a business has to obey.

2.3.C

Is our hypothesis right? Primary research

2.3.C.2
Business hypothesis

A statement of an assumption about a customer, product, or market. Businesses typically test whether their hypotheses hold up before committing to a course of action.

A handy template: “We think [these customers] will [do this] because [reason].” The best hypotheses include something you can check. “At least 40% of students would buy one every week” can be proven wrong. “Students will like it” can’t.

Choosing a method

Businesses test hypotheses with six primary research methods (2.3.C.1). They pick the method, or a mix of methods, based on the type of data they need (2.3.C.3):

2.3.C.4
When you need

a large amount of quantitative data that reflects the views of a population

Survey

The same questions to many people. numbers

2.3.C.5
When you need

in-depth qualitative data from a small number of people, like highly engaged or potential customers

Focus group or one-on-one interview

A focus group is a small group discussing together, guided by a moderator. An interview is one conversation. Both allow follow-up questions that draw out detailed explanations. words

2.3.C.6
When you need

data on customer behavior, not opinions

Experiment or observation

An experiment watches how customers behave in a controlled environment the researcher sets up. An observation watches them in a natural environment, without changing anything.

2.3.C.7
When you need

authentic customer responses to two viable alternatives

A/B test (a type of experiment)

Real customers get version A or version B in a real-world environment, and the business compares what they do. numbers

2.3.C

Can you trust the answer?

A business almost never asks every customer. It asks a sample: a smaller group that’s supposed to stand in for everyone. To reduce the risk of skewed data, the sample has to be (2.3.C.8):

Who you ask

Appropriately populated

It should look like the population being studied. A biased sample leans toward one kind of person, like your friends or a brand’s fans. In a random sample, everyone has the same chance of being picked.

How many you ask

Sufficiently large

Ask 10 people and each answer moves the result by 10 points. Ask more people and the result settles down.

Survey lab · Ridgeview High, 400 students

The takeaway: ask the right people, and enough of them. A bigger sample fixes the bouncing, but it doesn’t fix bias. Asking all 30 of your friends just gives you the same wrong answer every time.

Ask neutral questions

The other half of avoiding skewed data is how you ask (2.3.C.8). A leading question pushes people toward an answer, so the results tell you about your wording, not your customers. Neutral phrasing makes every answer, including “no,” easy to give.

LeadingNeutral
“Don’t you love our new smoothie?”“How would you rate our new smoothie from 1 to 5?”
“Most students say lunch costs too much. Do you agree?”“Is the price of lunch too low, about right, or too high?”
“How much did you enjoy our friendly staff?”“How would you describe your experience with our staff?”

Supported, not supported, or can’t tell?

The last step is reading the results honestly. Sometimes the honest answer is that the research can’t tell you yet: too few people, the wrong people, more than one change at a time, or a measure that doesn’t match the hypothesis.

What does the research say? 0 of 0

Research can still miss. Even good research can’t always predict how customers and markets will respond, so businesses often have to act on limited, unclear, or contradictory data (2.3.C.9). New Coke is the classic case: in 1985, Coca-Cola replaced its original formula with a sweeter one after taste tests showed many people preferred it. Customers protested losing the original, and within three months the company brought it back as Coca-Cola Classic. The taste tests answered “which tastes better?” but not “how will you feel if we take the original away?”
As a shopper

Ads quote research too: “9 out of 10 users love it,” “rated #1 by customers.” Run the same checks you used in the lab:

  • Who was asked? Customers who already bought it, the company’s own employees, or a random sample?
  • How many? 10 people or 10,000?
  • How was it asked? “Don’t you love it?” isn’t a fair question.
  • Who paid for it? A study paid for by the company selling the product deserves a second look.
