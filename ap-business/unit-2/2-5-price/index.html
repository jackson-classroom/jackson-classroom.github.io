2.5.A

Choosing a price: pricing strategies

Pricing strategy

A way of determining how much to charge for a product. Getting the price right is critical to whether a business survives, because price affects how many customers it attracts and keeps, its revenue, and its profit.

Every pricing decision starts with one number: the per-unit cost, which is what it costs to produce and distribute one unit of the good or service.

The rule that never changes. A low price may help a business gain market share, but a product is not profitable if its price is equal to or lower than its per-unit cost. Sell a $20 hoodie for $20 and you earn nothing. Sell it for $18 and you lose $2 on every sale.

Four common strategies

Value-based

Start from what customers think it’s worth

The price matches the value customers see in the product, even if that’s far above what it costs to make.

FitsHighly differentiated or one-of-a-kind products.

Competitive

Start from what rivals charge

The price is set at, just under, or just over the price of rival goods.

FitsProducts that are a lot like the competition’s.

Cost-based

Start from what it costs

Take the per-unit cost and add the profit the business wants on each unit (a markup).

FitsBusinesses that know their costs well and want a steady profit on every sale.

Penetration

Start low to get in

A new product launches at a low price, sometimes even below cost, to win customers and market share fast. The price goes up later.

FitsA new product entering a market that already has rivals.

As a shopper

Penetration pricing is aimed at you. A streaming service that advertises “$5 a month” for the first 3 months and then charges $15 costs $150 for the first year (3 × $5 + 9 × $15), not $60. Before you sign up, find the price you’ll pay after the introductory deal ends, and decide whether it’s still worth it at that price.

2.5.B

How high can it go? Pricing power

A pricing strategy is the plan. Pricing power is how much freedom a business has to carry it out.

Pricing power

A business’s ability to raise its price without losing many customers.

Three things decide how much pricing power a business has:

Competition

How many rivals?

Few rivals means more pricing power. When customers have many places to buy, a price increase sends them elsewhere.

Differentiation

How different is it?

A product customers see as unique, or a brand they’re loyal to, is hard to replace. A product just like the others isn’t.

Price sensitivity

How do customers react?

If customers barely change what they buy when the price changes, the business has more room to raise it.

2.5.C

What’s off limits: legal limits on pricing

Businesses are mostly free to choose their own prices, but the law draws three lines.

Collusion

Rivals agreeing on a price

Competitors secretly agree on what to charge instead of competing. Illegal in the United States and many other countries, because customers lose the benefit of competition.

Price gouging

Cashing in on a crisis

Sharply raising the price of something people urgently need during an emergency, like water after a hurricane. Illegal in many U.S. states and other countries.

Price discrimination

Charging by who you are

Charging customers different prices because of a protected status such as race, nationality, or sex. Illegal.

What’s still legal. Matching a rival’s price on your own isn’t collusion; the agreement is what breaks the law. Raising prices because your costs went up isn’t gouging. And not every price difference is illegal discrimination: matinee tickets, coupons, and student discounts aren’t based on a protected status.
