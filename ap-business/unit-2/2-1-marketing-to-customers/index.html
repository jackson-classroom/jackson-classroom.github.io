2.1.A

Why and how marketers collect customer data

2.1.A.1
Marketing

All of the activities businesses undertake to identify customers’ problems, needs, and wants, and to promote, sell, and deliver products.

Marketing isn’t just ads. It starts before the product exists, with figuring out who has a problem worth solving. That’s why marketers want data about customers. Customer data helps a business answer three questions (2.1.A.2):

01

Which customers should we serve?

Some groups need the product more, can afford it, and are easier to reach.

02

Which products should we make?

Data shows which features, sizes, and price points customers actually want.

03

How do we market them profitably?

Knowing where customers spend time and what they care about keeps marketing from being wasted.

Two kinds of customer data

Marketers sort customer characteristics into two groups (2.1.A.3). The quickest test: could you check it on a form or a census? Then it’s probably demographic. Is it about how someone thinks, spends their time, or what they care about? Then it’s psychographic.

Demographic
Measurable qualities used to describe a population.
agesexraceethnicityincomelocationeducationoccupation

Answers who and where: “a 16-year-old in suburban Denver whose household earns $90,000.”

Psychographic
Cognitive and behavioral factors: how people think and live.
interestsactivitiesvalueslifestylesattitudes

Answers why: “plays club volleyball, cares about sustainability, and shops secondhand.”

Watch-out: the same topic can land in either group depending on how it’s worded. “Lives 2 miles from a gym” is demographic (location). “Works out five days a week” is psychographic (an activity). Ask what is actually being measured.

How businesses get the data

Customer data comes from three places (2.1.A.4). Most of it is collected quietly, in the background of things you already do online.

Digital tools
  • Subscriber lists: email and text sign-ups
  • Online accounts: profiles and purchase history
  • Click-tracking software: what you click, view, and skip
  • Tracking apps: location and in-app behavior
  • Social media monitoring: posts, mentions, and hashtags about a brand
Traditional research tools
  • Surveys: the same questions asked of many people
  • Interviews: in-depth conversations with a few people
Buying it
  • Purchased data: businesses also buy customer data from other businesses, such as data brokers or partner companies

2.1.B

Segmentation, target customers, and customer profiles

Market segmentation

Grouping potential customers into market segments based on shared demographic and psychographic characteristics.

Target customers

The buyers most likely to purchase a specific product because of their wants, needs, and preferences.

Customer profile

A fictional description of one specific sample customer, with demographic and psychographic data plus their wants, needs, and preferences.

Segmentation helps a business understand what different groups need and how its product might help them (2.1.B.1). Then it picks the segment most likely to buy (2.1.B.2) and turns that segment into a person it can picture: a customer profile (2.1.B.3).

Why bother? When a business designs its product, branding, pricing, and advertising for its target customers, its marketing is typically more effective and less costly than trying to appeal to everyone (2.1.B.4). The lab below lets you see why.

    2.1.C

    Building customer relationships

    Finding customers is expensive. Keeping them is where the money is. Businesses build strong relationships with tactics like these (2.1.C.1), and social media and the internet make every one of them easier:

    Personalized service

    “The usual?”

    Remembering names, orders, sizes, and preferences, in person or through an online account.

    Rewards programs

    Buy 9, get 1 free

    Points, punch cards, and member perks that reward frequent buyers for coming back.

    Feedback opportunities

    “How did we do?”

    Satisfaction surveys, reviews, and comment cards, followed by actually fixing what customers report.

    Strong relationships pay off in two ways, and each has a number attached:

    2.1.C.2
    Customer acquisition cost (CAC)

    What it costs, on average, to win one new customer. Satisfied customers refer new customers for free, which pushes CAC down.

    CAC = total marketing, advertising, and sales costs to acquire customers ÷ number of customers acquired
    2.1.C.3
    Lifetime value of a customer

    The estimated amount of money one customer will spend on the business’s products over time. Loyal customers make repeat purchases, which pushes lifetime value up.

    One common estimate: lifetime value ≈ average purchase × purchases per year × years as a customer

    Lower CAC and higher lifetime value can both potentially increase profits. Only count costs spent to win customers in CAC. Rent, ingredients, and payroll for production aren’t acquisition costs.

    2.1.D

    When customer data becomes a risk

    Everything in this topic runs on customer data. That data carries risk for customers and for the business that holds it.

    Privacy infringement

    Collected without real consent

    Searches, purchases, card numbers, posts, and location can violate privacy, especially when customers don’t know data is being collected or how it will be used. (2.1.D.1)

    Data breach

    Stored without real security

    Data that isn’t properly secured can be stolen, leaving customers and businesses open to fraud and identity theft. (2.1.D.2)

    Business risk

    The cost of getting it wrong

    Businesses must weigh data’s benefits against losing customers, violating core values, and harm to reputation. (2.1.D.3)

    TermWhat it meansExample
    FraudSomeone uses stolen information to deceive or steal, usually money.Stolen card numbers are used to buy gift cards.
    Identity theftSomeone uses your personal information to act as you.A thief opens a credit card or files a tax return in your name.
    Privacy infringementPersonal information is collected, shared, or used in ways you didn’t knowingly agree to.A flashlight app quietly sells your location history.
    Personal finance link · Managing Risk

    You’re the customer in this story too. A few habits limit what you expose:

    • Check app permissions. A game doesn’t need your location or contacts.
    • Use a different strong password for important accounts, and turn on two-step verification.
    • Read what you’re agreeing to before trading your email or phone number for a discount.
    • Watch your bank and card statements for charges you don’t recognize.
    • Once you have credit, check your credit reports for free at AnnualCreditReport.com. You can also freeze your credit for free with each of the three credit bureaus.
