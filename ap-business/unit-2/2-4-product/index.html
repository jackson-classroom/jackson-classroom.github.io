The Product

2.4.A

From idea to launch: product development

A product is what a business offers to solve a customer’s problem or meet a need or want. It can be a good you can hold, like a pair of sneakers, or a service someone does for you, like cleaning them.

Getting from an idea to something customers can actually buy happens in stages. Businesses typically work through six:

  1. 1IdeationCome up with the idea.

    Market research, technical research and development (R&D), and brainstorming.

  2. 2ValidationCheck that customers want it.

    Test the idea with real customers, often with an MVP, before spending big.

  3. 3DesignDecide exactly what it will be.

    Its features, look, and materials, shaped by what validation taught.

  4. 4MessagingDecide how to talk about it.

    The overall marketing strategy, including the value proposition: why customers should choose it.

  5. 5ProductionFigure out how to make it.

    Make it the same way every time, at a cost that leaves room for profit.

  6. 6LaunchGet it to customers.

    Take orders and distribute the final product.

It’s not a straight line. Development is iterative: what a business learns at one stage can send it back to an earlier one. Testers say the backpack is too heavy? Back to design. Production costs too much? Back to design, or even back to the idea.

Testing the idea: the MVP

Validation is where a business finds out whether its idea holds up with real customers. A common tool is an MVP.

Minimum viable product (MVP)

The simplest version of a product that real customers can actually use and pay for. Businesses may use an MVP to assess whether a product can achieve product-market fit.

Product-market fit

Occurs if customer demand is sufficient to generate profit.

Minimum

Nothing extra

Only what it takes to solve the core problem. It should be quick and cheap to build, so a flop costs little.

Viable

Real enough to buy

Customers can actually use it and pay for it. Asking “would you buy this?” isn’t the same as watching people buy.

2.4.B

Making it recognizable: branding

Once a product exists, customers need a way to recognize it and tell it apart from rival goods that do the same job. That’s what a brand is for.

2.4.B.3
Brand identity

Can be conveyed through a name, idea, term, symbol, design, or a combination of these elements, which businesses often protect by trademarking.

A trademark gives a business the legal right to stop rivals from using its name, logo, or other brand elements on similar products. Without one, a copycat could sell a look-alike product and confuse customers.

Why businesses invest in a brand

Recognition

Easy to find

Shoppers spot it fast and know what they’re getting before they read a word.

Differentiation

Stands apart

It looks and sounds different from rival goods that do the same job.

Loyalty

Brings customers back

Customers who trust the name keep buying it, and many will pay more for it.

Brand or generic?

In addition to marketing products under brand names, businesses may sell generic products, because some customers care more about the cost savings that come with generic goods than about branding (2.4.B.4).

Store brands are the most common example: a grocery or drug store’s own cereal, paper towels, or pain reliever, usually on the shelf right next to the name brand. The store spends little on advertising and packaging, so it can charge less.
