Consumer Behavior

Why do people buy what they buy? Some of it is need, some is want, some is habit, and some is a marketer nudging at just the right moment. Businesses study these forces to build their marketing and sales tactics. Knowing them makes you a sharper business owner and a harder sell as a shopper.

2.2.A

Why people buy, and why they buy this one

2.2.A.1
From the course framework

“Consumers buy goods and services to meet basic needs and wants. When rival goods that meet the same needs and wants are available, consumer decisions regarding which products to buy are influenced by prices, differentiated product features, availability, and advertising.”

Needs
What you have to have to live and get through your life.
foodshelterclothinghealth caregetting to school or work
Wants
What makes life better or more fun, but you could live without.
concert ticketsthe newest phoneeating outdesigner labels

Most purchases mix the two. You need shoes; you want the $180 pair. Businesses sell to both.

Rival goods: same job, different products

Rival goods are different products that meet the same need or want: two brands of running shoes, or a bus pass and a bike. When rivals compete for your money, four things decide which one wins:

Price

What it costs compared with the others.

The store brand is $3 cheaper.
Differentiated features

What sets it apart: quality, design, extras.

This bottle keeps drinks cold for 24 hours.
Availability

Whether you can get it where and when you need it.

It’s the only one in stock today.
Advertising

Whether you’ve heard of it, and what you’ve heard.

You saw the ad ten times this week.

2.2.A

Think it through, or just grab it?

Not every purchase gets the same amount of thought (2.2.A.2). A rational process helps you find the product that best meets your needs and wants, but it isn’t free: it costs time spent gathering information.

Rational decision-making
Systematically comparing the alternatives, using the information you can get.

Used for major purchases: a home, a car, a laptop, a phone plan. It costs something: time and effort spent gathering information.

Rational doesn’t mean perfect. It means comparing the options on purpose instead of grabbing the first one.

Habitual (routine) decisions
Buying the same thing the same way, without much thought.

Common for small, repeated purchases: coffee on the way to school, your usual toothpaste. It saves time, and that makes sense when comparing would cost more than it could save.

Habits are great news for the brand you’re loyal to, and a tough wall for its rivals to get past.

Habits can flip. A routine purchase turns rational again when something changes: the price jumps, your income drops, or you learn something new, like a recall on your usual brand.

What shapes the choice: four kinds of influence

Two people can stand in front of the same shelf and make different choices. The course framework sorts the reasons into four groups. Each one shapes something different.

2.2.A.3
Personal
agesexeducationincome levelbudgetoccupationlifestyle

Shapes what you need and want, and what you can afford.

2.2.A.4
Psychological
values and beliefsperceptionslearningmotivations

Shapes your attitude toward a product and your willingness to buy. Learning means associations from past experience: the brand that broke on you twice.

2.2.A.5
Social and cultural
peersfamilysocial statuscultural normsmedia

Shapes what feels acceptable, desirable, or appropriate to buy.

2.2.A.6
Situational

Creates temporary conditions that encourage or discourage buying right now.

2.2.B

When laws and technology change how we buy

2.2.B.1
Purchasing pattern

A consumer’s typical routine for making purchases, including the timing, frequency, and quantity of purchases.

Timingwhen you buy
Frequencyhow often you buy
Quantityhow much you buy at once

Your pattern depends on where you live and work, your income, and the patterns of your family and friends. Two outside forces can change it:

Laws (2.2.B.2)

Regulate what you can buy, and when and where

Consumer protection laws keep fraudulent or unsafe products off the shelves. When a law makes a product harder to get, the need doesn’t go away, so people turn to substitute products.

Technology (2.2.B.3)

Makes new products and new ways to shop accessible

The internet in the 1990s and smartphones in the 2010s changed how people shop, find information, and get entertainment. Streaming replaced buying DVDs; one-tap ordering makes people buy more often.

Technology that makes buying easier also makes spending easier. One-tap checkout, saved cards, automatic subscriptions, and “pay in 4” buttons all remove the pause where you’d normally ask, “Do I need this?” Put the pause back: check your subscriptions every few months and cancel the ones you forgot about.

2.2.C

Cialdini’s principles of influence

2.2.C.1
Principles of influence

Psychological factors, identified by psychologist Robert Cialdini, that cause people to comply with a request. Marketers build sales tactics on these principles to motivate consumers to buy.

There are seven. Learn the one-line “tell” for each, and you’ll start spotting them everywhere (2.2.C.2–2.2.C.8).

The four that get mixed up

Authority, liking, consensus, and unity all involve other people. Ask who is persuading you, and why it works:

PrincipleIt works because…Example
Authoritythe person is an expertA dentist recommends the toothpaste.
Likingyou like or relate to the personYour favorite creator loves the snack.
Consensuslots of people already chose it12,000 five-star reviews.
Unitythey’re “one of us”: you share an identity“Made by gamers, for gamers.”

Consistency vs. unity trips people up too. Consistency is about your self-image: “for health-conscious people like you” works because you want to act like the person you think you are. Unity is about belonging to a group: “join our members-only community” or “help us design the next flavor.”

Personal finance link · Spending

When you feel pressure to buy, name the principle, then ask:

  • Scarcity: Would I still want it if there were plenty left?
  • Authority: Who is this expert, and were they paid to say it?
  • Consensus: Are the reviews real, and are those people like me?
  • Liking: Would I buy this if someone I didn’t care about were selling it?
  • Reciprocity: Do I owe them anything for a free sample? (No.)
  • Consistency: Is my streak or past choice still a good reason now?
  • Unity: Does being “one of us” require buying this?
